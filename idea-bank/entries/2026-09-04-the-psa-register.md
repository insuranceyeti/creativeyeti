# The PSA register — call before you shop

## Idea

Frame the message as a public service announcement rather than as an offer, and pay it off with an itemised list where every item carries a specific number or a specific product name. The trigger word "PSA" recasts a sales message as a warning from a friend, which is precisely the register a brand with a trust problem in this category needs.

## Spark

"PSA" turns a pitch into a warning, and it is the one register this brand's legitimacy problem actually rewards.

## Source read

**@paumod, posted 2026-01-08. 916,200 views, 81,400 likes, 20,400 shares, 599 comments, 52,376 saves.** Save rate **5.72%, the second highest in the library and more than four times the 1.31% middle.** Second largest view count in the pool. Seventy-three seconds.

A woman in her late twenties or early thirties in a cream fleece zip-up with gold jewellery, in a living room with daylight through blinds. Selfie-style handheld at eye level with visible minor shake — the shake reads as credibility rather than as a flaw. Direct eye contact throughout.

One high-contrast text block dominates the centre of frame for the full runtime: `PSA IF YOU'RE PREGNANT do this before you start shopping`. Two type treatments in one block, serif and sans mixed. It names the audience, makes a promise, and stakes an urgency claim with the word "before." At fifty-four seconds a single second overlay appears and is the only other thing on screen for the whole video.

Spoken: *"If you're watching this and you're pregnant, don't shop for anything until you call your health insurance."*

**There is no product, no document, no cutaway and no demonstration anywhere in it.** She lists breast pumps, milk storage bags, compression socks and a belly band and shows none of them. The four payoffs are the engine and they follow one rule: each carries a specific number or a specific product name. *"My insurance covered 70% of it, I think I paid maybe 30, 40 dollars out of pocket."* Then *"My insurance sent me a hundred a month, more than I could ever get through, 100% covered."* The percentages descend in specificity and rise in generosity, and the pacing accelerates as the list runs.

Then the hedge, and this is the part to copy in shape: *"Every health insurance is different, yours may not cover anything at all but it's worth a quick check."*

## Winning elements

- **hook** — Demographic fused to a direct command, with "PSA" as the framing trigger word.
- **script** — the itemised list where each item carries a number or a name.
- **offer** — "worth a quick check" as the ask, which is smaller and more believable than "sign up."
- **angle** — you already have coverage and you do not know what it pays for.

## Justification

The hedge is the finding. She undercuts her own promise and the video still holds a 5.72% save rate. That is direct evidence that a qualifier does not cost you the save in this category, which matters enormously for a brand whose rules force "up to 30%" rather than a flat number.

The brand has tested the trigger-word move and knows what it costs: `Moms43 - 5 - V1` runs on the word "hack" and produced 243 leads at $27.72 on a 38.91% hook rate and an 8.05% hold, with the highest CPM among the account's big spenders — so the trigger word stops people here and the cost problem sat in delivery rather than in the opener.

## Stage of awareness and audience fit

Solution aware, `inferred`. **This is the pregnancy-adjacent reference that is inside the product envelope**, and that is why it was selected over any of the twelve pregnancy videos in the library: it is not aimed at getting pregnant women covered, it is aimed at women who already have coverage and do not know what it pays for. The brand's guardrail — *"No pregnancy-only targeting — focus on moms with kids, not expectant mothers exclusively"* — does not touch it.

## Brand fit and risk

Fits. The risk is the pacing: it is a fast list, and slowing it to explain the brand's model would be exactly the drift `adapting-scripts.md` warns against. And the payoffs must be things the brand can actually stand behind — the compliant version swaps the itemised list for the brand's own approved claims, not invented ones.

## Notes

Sits close to the largest trap in this vault. Twelve of the nineteen Relevant videos are anchored to pregnancy and the brand's lifetime library returns zero matches for "pregnant" — but 42 comments across 15 ads over fourteen months are women reporting they were told they do not qualify because they are pregnant. That gap is a product gate, not whitespace. This entry threads it deliberately.
