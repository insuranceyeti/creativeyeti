# One family in every seven

**concept_name:** One family in every seven

**idea:** A 1954 Blue Shield magazine ad sold hospital coverage by naming odds rather than a price. One family in every seven gets a surgery bill. The mechanic is a headline that puts a number on the risk instead of on the product, and it is worth holding here for one specific reason: this account's audience beats every price figure it is shown, and an odds figure is not a number she can counter-bid.

**source_link:** https://www.ebay.de/itm/294964707937 (an archive marketplace listing, which is where the headline was recorded, and which is the only viewable reference found)

**source_path:** `parker-system/creative-strategy-context/old-ads/INDEX.md`, which is scaffolded and holds zero entries as of 2026-09-04, so live web archives were used as the fallback the corpus README specifies.

**source_read:** **The ad image could not be opened from this session and no visual reconstruction is offered.** Three archive and marketplace hosts were tried and every one is blocked by this environment's network egress proxy: `repository.duke.edu`, `www.ebay.de` and `adretro.com`. What was recoverable is the listing's own description of the ad, which records a **1954 Blue Shield / Blue Cross magazine advertisement whose headline is a frequency statistic: one family in every seven will get a surgery bill.** The listing is in German and translates the headline rather than reproducing the English original, so the exact English wording is not established.

I am not going to describe the illustration, the layout or the body copy, because I have not seen them. Per the source-capture standard in this system, a label with a caveat is still a failed creative read, so this entry claims a headline mechanic and nothing else.

**justification:** Thin, and the thinness is the whole story of this entry.

The evidence is a single archive listing title. There is no view of the ad, no publication issue, no page, and no second source. `parker-system/creative-strategy-context/old-ads/README.md` says old ads justify on craft and durability rather than on a metric, which is the right bar, and this entry cannot even fully meet that bar because the craft cannot be inspected.

What keeps it in the bank is the shape and the brand's own problem. This account's single most reliable audience behavior is beating a number. **82 of 1,342 comments open with "mine is," 76 of those name a figure, and 64 of the 76, or 84%, name a number above the ad's $6,000** [`verified`, `personas/voice-of-customer/voc-pain-phrase.md`]. A headline that states how often something happens rather than what it costs does not offer that handle. Whether that actually helps is untested, and the entry says so.

The second reason to keep it is that it is the only entry in this bank from the historian lane, and an empty lane is worth marking with something honest rather than leaving blank.

**Evidence picture.** One listing title, no viewable ad, no second source. Confidence: **data-limited**, which is a stronger caveat than thin. Do not brief from this entry without opening the ad first.

**stage_of_awareness:** Unaware to problem aware. An odds headline is aimed at somebody who has not yet decided the risk applies to her. [`inferred`.]

**persona_or_audience_fit:** Unverified. Nothing about a 1954 magazine reader maps onto this brand's audience map, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the forced fit the articulation test warns against. [`data-limited`.]

**brand_fit:** Untested and probably narrow. This brand's compliance rules require every claim to be substantiated, and nothing anywhere in this brain says what the brand's own "up to 30%" figure compares against [`verified`, `sub-context-docs/brand-identity-analysis.md`]. A frequency statistic would need a real, citable source before it could run, and this brand currently has none for the claims it already makes.
