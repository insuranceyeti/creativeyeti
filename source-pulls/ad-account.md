# Ad account — persona signal — Health For Moms

**How this read was built.** I opened the account cold and ranked the last 90 days by spend, because in a lead-gen account Meta's own budget allocation is the closest thing to a verdict on which creative is working. That window runs 2026-06-05 to 2026-09-02 and holds $98,276.81 of spend, 4,336 leads and a $22.67 CPL across 117 ad-name groups. Then I did the thing that matters most for persona work: I stopped reading the numbers and watched the ads. Thirteen of them, all the way through, with the transcript, the AI creative analysis and the creator demographic in hand, because who a creative serves lives in the talent, the room, the child on screen and the first three seconds, not in the ad name. After that I pulled the age, gender and platform delivery split for each of those thirteen separately, so I could hold my read of who an ad courts against who Meta actually found. Finally I went hunting for absence. I filtered the library for statics, and I ran four semantic sweeps looking for the buyers the brand says it wants: the single mom, the self-employed mom, the mom of teenagers, the woman over fifty, the creator who is not white.

Two things reshaped the read along the way, and both are worth flagging up front because a faster pass would have gotten them wrong.

First, I nearly filed a false gap. The brand says its winner is the state angle. The top-spending ad is a POV video about telling your husband you saved $400 a month, so on the surface the brand's story and the account disagree. It does not. When I read the transcript, the state list is inside that ad, at 0:33, and the ad set is literally named `Moms43 - 4 (State Moment)`. The state gate is the closing mechanism in all thirteen ads I watched. The brand's claim is true. What the brand's claim leaves out is that the state list is the qualifier, not the hook, and the wrapper around it is doing something very specific to a very specific woman.

Second, the account has a whole earlier chapter the 90-day window hides. Statics ran 529 ads and $168,907.73 for 13,735 leads at a $12.30 CPL, mostly through 2025. Video has run 1,593 ads and $574,310.36 for 25,834 leads at $22.23. Statics are 22.7% of lifetime spend and 34.7% of lifetime leads. That does not prove statics are better, and I say why below, but it does mean the current video-only picture is one chapter of a two-chapter account, and reading only the recent window would have made the served audience look narrower and more settled than it is.

The bias to hold over everything that follows: this account shows who the creative chose to court and who Meta then found. It does not show who bought, because in this business nobody buys here. A lead is a phone number handed to a partner agency. A winning message proves only that someone was willing to trade her number for a state-list check.

## Served-audience signals

The account courts four distinct kinds of woman. One of them absorbs nearly all the money. The other three are visible in the creative but starved of spend, which makes them served in name and unserved in practice.

**1. The partnered, already-insured mom of babies and toddlers who suspects she is overpaying. Inferred, strong.**

This is the audience the account is actually built for, and it is not close. She is married or partnered, she already has a plan through a job, and the ad's whole premise is that her existing coverage is worse than she thinks. The creative never asks whether she has insurance. It assumes she does and reframes it as a bad deal.

The evidence for who she is comes from the creative itself. In `Moms43 - 4 - V3`, the account's top spender at $42,741.16 in 90 days and $55,794.26 lifetime, the opening nine seconds are a mother and an infant lying in white bedding in soft beige light, looking into the camera, with an acoustic track singing "These memories will stay with me." No one speaks. The whole message sits in a text block over the picture: "POV: Telling your husband you found better health insurance, saved $400 a month, AND the deductible is zero. Wife of the year energy. 💕" Then it cuts to the same mother on a living room floor tickling two laughing toddlers. Only at 0:18 does a person talk, and it is a different woman entirely, a blonde spokesperson in her late forties or early fifties in a green floral dress who says: "Listen, this is why you can't simply just say, 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered.' No. You need health insurance that has a zero dollar deductible without spending thousands. And you can only get that if you are a mom and you live in one of these states."

Read that bundle as a set of signals aimed at a kind of person and the person is unmistakable. There is a husband to report the win to. There is a household budget she has authority over. There are small children in a calm, clean, well-lit home. The emotional payoff on offer is not relief from fear, it is credit. "Wife of the year energy" is a status line. Parker's own tag on this ad is Pride, and Pride carries 49.5% of the 90-day spend on only 7 tagged ads, which tells you how much money sits behind that one emotional register.

Meta's delivery backs the read. On that ad, 43.6% of spend landed on 25-34 and 41.2% on 35-44, with only 12.0% on 45 and over. Female 94.8%. Mobile 99.5%. So the platform found a young-to-middle mother, which is exactly who the creative is dressed for. Where the creative and the delivery agree like this, the read moves from my inference toward verified.

This audience is not one ad. It is a family of six ad groups all carrying that same husband hook: `Moms43 - 4 - V3`, `- V1`, `- V4`, `moms-63 2b`, `moms-63 3e` and `moms-63 2b - Copy`. Together they hold $66,668.60 of the 90-day spend, which is 67.8% of everything the account spent, and they produced 3,120 of the 4,336 leads, or 72.0%, at a blended $21.37 CPL.

**2. The same mom, but reached through grievance instead of aspiration, and she is meaningfully older. Inferred, strong, and this is the most useful finding in the doc.**

A second family of ads opens on a distressed face and a screenshot of a stranger's complaint. `Moms43 - 5 - V1` opens on a woman in a dark hoodie filming herself, framed as a social post from "Charlotte Rose @sunshinemom23" captioned "Sharing this 2026 health insurance hack!!", with a tweet from Cindie Hayner laid over her face: "My deductible is $6000. I have to pay $6000 before my insurance even kicks in...but yet...I pay $1000/mo for that insurance?! Make that make sense...." She spent $10,020.57 in 90 days for 425 leads at $23.58.

Same offer. Same state gate. Same product. And a completely different woman shows up.

On the aspirational POV ads, 45 and over takes 7.7% to 12.0% of spend. On the grievance ads it takes 30% to 38%. The exact figures, all verified from per-ad delivery data:

| Ad | Opening frame | 45+ share of spend | Facebook share |
|---|---|---|---|
| `MOMS34 - N1 - 3a` | silent lifestyle montage, no talking head | 6.9% | 84.4% |
| `moms-63 3e` | aspirational family montage, POV overlay | 8.8% | 44.7% |
| `moms-63 2b` | mom and son leaving a beautiful home | 8.9% | 40.9% |
| `Moms43 - 4 - V3` | mother and baby in bed | 12.0% | 58.4% |
| `moms-53 3` | Approved State List graphic, straight to camera | 17.0% | 90.7% |
| `moms54-3` | crying mother, tear-stained face | 29.9% | 92.8% |
| Black creator grievance ad | stressed woman, locs and glasses | 32.6% | 73.1% |
| `Moms43 - 5 - V1` | selfie plus $6,000 deductible tweet | 36.2% | 44.8% |
| single-mom static | avatar, "my insurance was failing us" | 38.1% | 87.3% |

That is a clean split and it is not a coincidence of one ad. Warm, calm, aspirational openers pull a woman in her late twenties and thirties. Angry, tearful, complaint-driven openers pull a woman in her forties and fifties. Both women get sold the identical $0 deductible plan behind the identical state list.

She converts worse on the metric the brand grades by. The grievance family, roughly fourteen ad groups, took $18,270 of 90-day spend, or 18.6%, and returned 688 leads at a $26.56 CPL. That is above the brand's own "not good" line of $25. But she watches far longer. Hold rate on the aspirational POV family runs 2.36% to 4.32%. On the grievance ads it runs 7.60% on `Moms43 - 5 - V1`, 13.66% on `moms54-3` and 17.12% on `MOMS38 - 1 - V1`, the "Health Insurance is a scam 🙄" ad that is still the single biggest lead producer in the account's history at 2,689 leads on $54,173.20 and a $20.15 CPL. Against the bar in `ad-account-analysis.md`, which wants hold rate at 12% and up, the account's biggest spender fails badly at 3.27% and the grievance ads are the only creative in the library that clears it.

So the honest read is not "grievance is worse." It is that the account has two audiences, and it is optimizing hard toward the cheaper one while the more engaged one gets a fifth of the money and no dedicated creative built for her.

**3. The mom who is here because another mom told her to. Inferred, thin on spend, strong on signal.**

A small set of ads drops the POV frame and the grievance frame entirely and runs on peer referral. `moms-53 3` opens on a woman in a grey rib-knit tank in front of a full-screen "Approved State List ✔️" and says, "I'm so thankful for the mom that told me about this. If you're a mom and you live in one of these states and you're tired of paying high deductibles, high premiums for health insurance for your family, listen up." The creator is a woman in her early thirties, mixed race or Hispanic, with a nose piercing and visible arm tattoos. She spent $1,761 in 90 days for 110 leads at $16.01 CPL, and she put up a 3.15% CTR and a $0.93 cost per link click, the strongest click economics anywhere in the account. Delivery went 90.7% Facebook.

A sibling ad, `moms-63 1f`, runs the same idea as a POV line: "POV: You shared Health for Moms in the group chat and now three friends have better insurance because of you. Legend behavior.💕" It has only $29.28 of spend and 2 leads, so it proves nothing on its own, but it shows the referral idea is being reached for more than once.

This matters because the brand's own context document names peer recommendation as a tipping point that "can collapse the decision timeline from weeks to minutes." The account has tested it twice at under $1,800 combined and gotten its best click economics both times.

**4. The uninsured mom who fell through the cracks. Present in the library, absent from the spend.**

The account has made this creative. A 2025 video, ad `120227093360330519`, has a woman in a black denim jacket saying: "There's a part of being a mom no one really warns you about. You're taking care of everyone else but you have no safety net. I was working, raising my daughter, doing everything right, except I didn't have health insurance. No job coverage, not eligible for assistance, and the affordable plans? Yeah, they weren't. I kept pushing it off until a minor emergency turned into a 1,000 dollar bill." It closes on "If you feel like you've fallen through the cracks, this is the bridge."

That is a different buyer with a different trigger, and the script is genuinely good. It got $67.51 of spend and 1 lead. A companion static headlined "I'm a single mom, and I didn't know my insurance was failing us... 😭" got $1,009.79 and 67 leads at a $15.07 CPL, which beats the account average, and it is paused. Combined lifetime spend on this audience is $1,077.30, or about 0.14% of the account's $743,218 lifetime total.

I am marking her as a served-audience signal rather than an absence because the creative exists and one execution beat the account average. But nothing was learned, because Meta was never given enough budget to learn anything.

## Message-to-buyer map, frequency-ranked

Ranked by how heavily each message recurs across the winning creative and how much spend sits behind it.

**1. "You are overpaying, and moms in your state can get a $0 deductible plan." Present in 13 of 13 ads read at full media depth. Roughly 92.7% of 90-day spend.**

This is not one message among several. It is the account's entire conversion architecture, and every other message in this list is a wrapper around it. The mechanism is identical every time: name the pain, promise a $0 deductible, cut to a full-screen "Approved State List" of 32 to 34 states, tell her it takes 30 seconds. Verified across every transcript I pulled. The buyer it implies is a woman who already has coverage and can be persuaded she is paying too much for too little. That is inferred from the fact that not one of the thirteen scripts asks whether she is insured.

Worth naming for compliance, since section 8 of the brand document calls these rules non-negotiable: the winning scripts hold the line. They say "save up to 30%," they say "$0 deductible option" and "may be able to get," and they carry an on-screen dramatization disclaimer. The state gate is doing double duty as a legal qualifier and a curiosity hook.

**2. The spousal win. 6 ad groups, $66,668.60, 67.8% of 90-day spend, 3,120 leads, $21.37 CPL.**

"POV: Telling your husband you found better health insurance, saved $400 a month, AND the deductible is zero. Wife of the year energy. 💕" This is the biggest real bet in the account by a wide margin and it is not close.

Two things about it are strategically interesting. It sells a status moment, not the product. The reward on offer is being seen as the one who fixed it. And it converts best in its newest, most aspirational cut: `moms-63 2b`, which opens on a well-dressed mother and son walking out of a beautiful home, hit a $13.29 CPL on $5,527.76 and 416 leads. That is the best result at real spend anywhere in the 90 days and it clears the brand's own "fantastic" bar of under $15. Its sibling `moms-63 3e` hit $15.14. Meanwhile the original bed-and-baby cut is running at $22.21 on ten times the budget.

The buyer this win implies is a partnered woman with household financial authority and a spouse whose opinion is a reward worth earning. Inferred from the creative. The delivery data confirms only the age and gender, not the marital status, so I am holding the partnered read as mine.

**3. The high-deductible grievance. Roughly 14 ad groups, $18,270, 18.6% of 90-day spend, 688 leads, $26.56 CPL.**

Anchored on one recurring artifact: a screenshot of a tweet reading "My deductible is $6000. I have to pay $6000 before my insurance even kicks in...but yet...I pay $1000/mo for that insurance?! Make that make sense...." It appears in at least six separate ads I read. The spoken frames vary: "This is why you can't simply just say 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered'"; "Can we stop acting like any health insurance is good health insurance?"; "Health Insurance is a scam 🙄."

This is the message the brand runs heavily that wins a narrower and older audience than the spend implies it is chasing. It is also the message with the deepest attention, and the account's all-time lead leader sits inside it.

**4. Peace of mind and the end of medical worry. Roughly $5,500, 5.6% of 90-day spend.**

"POV: At peace...because my family finally has the best health insurance and I pay less. ❤️" over a mother playing hide and seek with a toddler in a sunlit hallway, with no dialogue at all until a voiceover at 0:27 says "Finally, insurance that actually feels like it has our back." `MOMS34 - N1 - 3a` ran $5,359.15 in the window for 208 leads at $25.77, and $26,459.63 lifetime for 1,282 leads at $20.64. A newer cut promises "No Googling symptoms at 2am. No worrying about 'surprise bills'. JUST PEACE."

This message pulls the youngest audience in the account. On `MOMS34 - N1 - 3a`, 49.6% of spend landed on 25-34 and only 6.9% on 45 and over. The buyer it implies is a mother of very young children whose dominant feeling about health care is low-grade dread rather than sticker shock.

**5. Mom-to-mom referral. Roughly $1,790, 1.8% of 90-day spend, best click economics in the account.**

Covered above. Ranked fifth by spend and second by CTR. That inversion is the point.

**6. The system is rigged. Roughly $550, well under 1%.**

A small claymation set: "Did you know your bill is cheaper if you don't use your insurance?", "Same scan, same hospital. One price for cash, a much bigger one if you hand over your card," "Your insurance company and your hospital are often the same company now." Hold rates of 11.15% to 13.59%, well above the account norm, on $86 to $382 of spend each. Barely funded, and it keeps the villain on the system rather than a named company, which is exactly what the brand's own guardrails require.

**7. Fell through the cracks, the uninsured. $1,077 lifetime, effectively unfunded.**

**The mismatch worth naming.** The brand's biggest bet, the spousal win, is aimed at a partnered woman with a spouse and a budget to manage. Two of the brand's five stated ICPs, Marissa the single mother and Courtney the self-employed one, have no husband in the frame at all. If those buyers are real, 67.8% of the spend is speaking past them by construction, not by accident.

## Spend concentration and what it serves

This account is extraordinarily concentrated, more than any read of the ad count would suggest.

- 117 ad-name groups spent money in the last 90 days.
- The top 13 groups took $91,097.19, which is **92.7% of all spend**.
- The single top group took $42,741.16, which is **43.5% of all spend**.
- By group 21 in the ranking, spend is down to $413.62. By group 40 it is $15.59.

So the tail is not a testing operation. It is a graveyard. Roughly a hundred ad-name groups shared about $7,180 between them in three months, which averages under $72 each. Meta cannot learn from $72, so almost every angle the brand thinks it has tested has not actually been tested. That is the most important context for everything the brand believes about what works.

**What the concentration serves.** Spend allocation is a bet on the aspirational, partnered, young mother of babies and toddlers, made whether or not anyone chose it. She takes 67.8% of spend through the spousal-win family alone. Account-wide delivery lands 38.6% of spend on 25-34 and 42.6% on 35-44, so **81.2% of the money reaches women 25 to 44**, with 12.7% on 45-54 and 3.5% on 55 and over. Female 95.2%. Mobile 99.5%. Facebook 60.2%, Instagram 39.2%.

**What it starves.** The older mom who watches the grievance ads for three to five times longer than anyone watches the winners gets 18.6% of the budget and no creative built specifically for her. The uninsured mom gets 0.14% of lifetime spend. The referral message, which produced the account's best CTR and cost per link click, gets 1.8%.

**The unintentional bet.** The brand's stated posture, from section 11 of its context document, is "We want to focus mostly on video ads. MAYBE some image ads are fine to find angles." The lifetime numbers say something else. Statics ran $168,907.73 across 529 ads for 13,735 leads at a **$12.30 CPL**. Video ran $574,310.36 across 1,593 ads for 25,834 leads at a **$22.23 CPL**. Statics are 22.7% of lifetime spend and 34.7% of lifetime leads.

I want to be careful here, because this is the kind of gap that gets over-read. The statics mostly ran in 2025 with CPMs between $13.69 and $22.06, against $22.98 in the current window, so a meaningful slice of that difference is a cheaper auction, not better creative. The landing page and the lead form may also have changed. And with lead quality invisible, a cheaper lead may simply be a worse one. What I can say plainly is that the account has a second format that historically produced a third of its leads at roughly half the cost, the brand has deprioritized it by stated preference, and nothing in the data I can reach explains why. The static headlines are also carrying persona angles the video never touches: "Didn't know I could ditch my job's health plan… until I did" at a $11.56 CPL, "One ER visit could've wiped us out" at $9.62, "Moms, your health insurance should cover preventative care..." at $7.17.

## Unserved audiences

Named as absences, because in this account an absence is usually a spend decision rather than a creative one.

**Mothers of school-age children and teenagers.** Across all 13 ads read at full media depth, the children on screen are infants and toddlers almost without exception. One script mentions school-age kids without showing them: "I'm currently waiting in line to pick up my girls from school." No teenager appears in any ad I watched. Meanwhile 42.6% of spend delivers to women 35-44 and 12.7% to 45-54, which is a large body of women whose children are well past the toddler stage watching ads full of babies. The brand's own stated testing priority is "All different ages of moms with ages of kids." The creative to run that test does not exist yet. Verified for the ads read; I cannot rule out a teenager somewhere in the 2,122-ad library.

**The self-employed mother.** The brand's Courtney ICP runs a business, is not on her husband's plan because adding a spouse costs $480 a month, and is sitting on a short-term plan she is "about 60% sure" covers anything. A semantic sweep for self-employment, small business ownership and having no employer plan surfaced nothing built for her. Every winning script assumes an existing plan she is overpaying on, which is a different situation entirely.

**The mother in a coverage transition.** New baby, maternity leave, a divorce, a job change, a benefits package "under review." The brand's own context document names these as the most common tipping points into the category. The account touches a newborn only once, in `moms54-3`, and even there it is framed as a claim-denial fight rather than a coverage change. There is no open enrollment creative in the window, which matters because November 1 is the brand's stated peak. Worth noting the brand has a standing guardrail against pregnancy-only targeting, so part of this absence is deliberate.

**Non-white mothers at any real spend.** The library is not all white. There is an ad led by a Black woman with locs and glasses saying "I pay fifteen hundred dollars a month out of pocket for health insurance, every single month for me and my child," then reacting to a $1,295 prescription with "This is not real life, y'all." There is an Asian-American creator leading a cost-of-living ad. The best-CTR ad in the account, `moms-53 3`, is led by a woman who reads as mixed race or Hispanic. But the first two got **$206.08 and $46.83 of lifetime spend**. Combined with a third older-mom test, all three total $287.12. The creative was made and then never funded, so the absence is in the budget, not the roster.

**Women over 50 as an addressed audience.** One ad exists, `MOMS 28 - FOLDER 2 - AD 2`, led by a woman in her mid-fifties: "At 55, I don't want complicated. I want clear, trustworthy, and done." It got $34.21 and is paused. Yet 45 and over takes 16.2% of 90-day spend and up to 38% on individual grievance ads. The account reaches this woman constantly and has never once spoken to her directly.

**Anyone who already knows the brand.** Parker's awareness tags put 56.2% of spend on Solution Aware and 37.1% on Problem Aware, with a single Unaware ad at $36.35 and nothing at all tagged Product Aware or Most Aware. For a business whose leads go to agents who may not reach them for days, there is no creative anywhere for the woman who filled out the form and did not answer the phone.

**The husband.** He is named in the hook that carries 67.8% of the spend and he never appears, is never spoken to, and is never given a reason to bring this up. The brand's own document says partners "may initiate the search" as the catalyst. Male delivery is 4.2% of spend, which is spill, not targeting.

## Served-versus-stated gap

Four gaps, each named on both sides and left open for the synthesis.

**The brand names five buyers. The spend funds roughly one.** Stated: Jen the family safety net, Danielle the overwhelmed planner, Marissa the single mother holding it together alone, Courtney the accidental entrepreneur, Nicole the second-chapter mom at 41. Served: a composite of Jen and Danielle, partnered, already insured, mother of small children, reachable through either pride or grievance. Marissa has $1,077 of lifetime spend against her name. Courtney and Nicole have no creative I could find built for their situations. This is the widest gap in the doc and the synthesis should weigh it hard, because four fifths of the stated persona set has never been funded.

**The brand describes its winner by its ending. The money is going to its beginning.** Stated, from section 9: "State angle has worked very well if you see the account," and from section 10: "Right now we're mainly on iterations of our state angle." Verified: true, the state gate is in every winner. But the state list is the qualifier at 0:24 to 0:33, and what varies between a $13.29 CPL and a $30.43 CPL on the exact same state list is the first ten seconds. `moms-63 2b` and `Moms43 - 4 - V4` carry word-for-word the same text hook and the same offer, and they differ by more than two to one on CPL. The difference is entirely in the opening visual: an aspirational walk out of a beautiful home versus a mother lying in bed with a baby. If the team believes the state angle is the variable, it is iterating on the constant.

**The brand grades itself "fantastic" under $15 and the account is sitting in "meh."** Stated tiers from section 11: under $15 fantastic, $15 to $20 good, $20 to $25 meh, over $25 not good. The 90-day account CPL is **$22.67**, which is meh by the brand's own scale, and the top spender at $22.21 is in the same band. Only two ads at real spend clear into good or better, `moms-63 2b` at $13.29 and `moms-63 3e` at $15.14, and both are recent and both carry under 6% of the budget. The account is scaling its middling performer and under-funding its best one.

**The brand says it has low creative diversity. The tag data says the concentration is even tighter than that.** Stated, and candidly: "But not a ton of creative diversity." Verified: POV format holds 69.8% of spend on 28 ads. Social Interface holds 15.8% and Callout 12.2%. Everything else is rounding. Skit took $683 on 10 ads, UGC Single $613 on 7, Educational $512 on 7, AI Animation $125 on 4, Green Screen $7.95 on 2, Podcast $5.38 on 1. On the desire tag, "Care and protection of loved ones" carries 86.5% of spend across 74 ads. So the account is not running three formats and one emotional register by accident of testing. It is running one format and one desire at scale, and calling the rest tested when the rest has never had a budget.

## Behavioral-signal states the creative leans on

These are situational states layered on a buyer, not identities. Every one of them can sit on top of any of the served audiences above, which is exactly why they should be held as overlays for the synthesis to attach rather than promoted into personas.

**The qualification scan.** The single most-used state in the account, present in all 13 ads read. A full-screen list of 32 to 34 states appears and the viewer stops to look for hers. It converts a passive watch into an active search in about two seconds, and it doubles as a scarcity frame: "Just remember that you have to live in one of those states, otherwise it's not gonna work." It is doing the work a discount usually does in a category where the brand has forbidden itself the word discount.

**The friction collapse.** "It'll take you 30 seconds." Present in nearly every script, always immediately after the state list. It is aimed squarely at the overwhelm objection the brand names in section 5, that shopping for a plan is "just too hard."

**The reportable household win.** The state activated by "Wife of the year energy" and "you shared Health for Moms in the group chat... Legend behavior." She is not being sold relief, she is being sold the moment she gets to tell someone. This is the state behind 67.8% of the spend.

**The objection pre-empt.** `moms-63 3e` opens its spoken section with "I almost scrolled past this because I thought, 'I already have health insurance, this doesn't apply to me,'" then closes with a risk reversal: "Worst case, you check and stick with what you have. Best case, you realize you've been paying way more than you needed to." Reading this through the TEEP model in `emotional-delivery-and-timing.md`, that is Evaluation-phase creative, naming the specific hesitation rather than adding pressure, and it is the only ad in the account doing it. It runs a $15.14 CPL.

**Sticker shock at renewal.** The $6,000 deductible tweet. A Trigger-phase state, mirroring her internal state back before offering anything.

**The claim-denial fight.** "Just got off a two hour call fighting for them to cover my newborn son's hospital stay. Just zero humanity, zero compassion." The highest-intensity state in the library, and it produces the highest hook rate at 57.02% and a 13.66% hold rate on $1,283.69 of spend.

**Cost-of-living squeeze.** "Groceries went from $150 a week to $220. Gas is up, our electric bill's insane." Present in one barely-funded ad. It reframes insurance as the one bill she can actually control, which is a genuinely different doorway from every other message here.

Worth naming through the emotional-delivery lens: the account's spend clusters in two zones and skips the middle. It runs high-arousal negative in the grievance ads and warm positive in the POV ads. The quiet, low-intensity, empathetic register, the one that doc says most brands underinvest in and that tends to work mid-funnel, is essentially absent. The closest thing to it is the silent lifestyle montage, which carries no argument at all.

## Recurrence and spread

**What I read.** Thirteen ads at full media depth, meaning transcript, AI creative analysis, creator demographic and per-ad delivery breakdown. Forty ad-name groups at hook, visual-hook and angle level from the 90-day spend ranking. Ten statics at headline and angle level. Forty-eight semantic report chunks across four sweeps. Account-wide tag distribution across all tagged ads in the window.

**Denominators.** The 90-day window is 2026-06-05 to 2026-09-02 and contains 117 ad-name groups, $98,276.81 of spend, 4,336 leads and a $22.67 CPL at a $22.98 CPM and a 1.80% CTR. The lifetime library is 1,593 video ads and 529 statics, $743,218.09 of combined spend and 39,569 leads.

**How heavily each message recurs, by share of 90-day spend.** State gate as closing mechanism, 13 of 13 ads read, roughly 92.7%. Spousal win, 6 groups, 67.8%. High-deductible grievance, roughly 14 groups, 18.6%. Peace of mind, 5.6%. Mom-to-mom referral, 1.8%. System-is-rigged, under 1%. Uninsured, 0.14% of lifetime.

**How heavily each audience recurs, by share of 90-day spend.** Partnered insured mom of small children, 67.8% through the spousal-win family alone and more once peace-of-mind is added. Older grievance-responsive mom, 18.6%. Referral-driven mom, 1.8%. Uninsured mom, effectively zero.

**How much rests on creative alone versus on performance data.** This is the part a reader should weigh most carefully.

*Backed by delivery data, approaching verified:* the age and platform split between aspirational and grievance openers, drawn from nine separate per-ad breakdowns and consistent across all nine. The account-wide skew to women 25-44 on mobile. The spend concentration figures. Every CPL, hook rate and hold rate quoted.

*Inferred from creative alone, mine to own:* that the served buyer is partnered, that she already has employer coverage, that she has household financial authority, and that the reward she is buying is status rather than relief. Meta reports age, gender, platform and device. It does not report marital status, insurance status or income, and I have not laundered those into facts.

*Stated, from the brand and unverified by me:* the five ICPs, the CPL tier definitions, the claim that the state angle is the proven winner, the claim that political angles do not work, and the description of the account as low-diversity.

*Not available at all:* whether any of these leads became customers. There is no purchase event, the `invitee_meeting_scheduled` conversion returned nothing in the window, and only 42 `Call` events fired against 4,336 leads. Every CPL comparison in this doc measures the cost of a phone number, not the cost of a customer. If the grievance ads produce a woman who answers the phone and the aspirational ads produce one who does not, the entire ranking above inverts, and nothing reachable from this account can tell you which is true.

**Confidence.** Strong on the served-audience split by opener type, because creative read and delivery data agree across nine ads. Strong on spend concentration and the format monoculture, because those are arithmetic. Mixed on the message-to-buyer map, because CPL is a weak proxy for a buyer when the buyer is invisible. Thin on every unserved audience claim, because absence of evidence in a 2,122-ad library sampled by semantic search is weaker than presence, and I have said where that applies.

**A note on refresh.** The refresh cadence doc puts persona source pulls on a 180-day tier. I have stamped this one at 30 days instead, because the prompt that generates it calls it among the fastest-refreshing docs in the system and this account bears that out: three of the four best-performing ads in the window were created within the last three weeks of it, and the two ads clearing the brand's "good" CPL bar did not exist in June. A 180-day stamp would let this go stale roughly five creative cycles before anyone checked it.

## Open loops

**1. The format the account walked away from.**

Statics ran 529 ads and $168,907.73 for 13,735 leads at a $12.30 CPL. Video has run 1,593 ads and $574,310.36 for 25,834 leads at $22.23. Statics are 22.7% of lifetime spend and 34.7% of lifetime leads, and the brand has since decided to "focus mostly on video ads."

*Pull: Surprise.* I expected the deprioritized format to be the weaker one, and instead the format the brand set aside is the one that historically produced leads at roughly half the cost.

*Question:* What is behind the gap between the cost per lead statics delivered and the cost per lead video delivers now?

*Why it is a loop:* If a meaningful part of that gap is creative rather than a cheaper 2025 auction or a lower-quality lead, then the cheapest growth available to this brand is a format it already knows how to make and has stopped making. If it is not, the team can stop wondering and commit to video without the doubt.

*Territory: Messaging.*

**2. The older woman who keeps showing up.**

Ads that open on a distressed face and a complaint screenshot deliver 30% to 38% of their spend to women 45 and over. Ads that open on a warm aspirational montage deliver 7% to 12% there. Same offer, same state list, same product behind both. She also watches three to five times longer, with hold rates of 7.6% to 17.1% against 2.4% to 4.3%.

*Pull: Pattern.* The same split showed up independently across nine separate per-ad delivery breakdowns, which is more than a quirk of one ad.

*Question:* Who is the older mom that the grievance creative keeps reaching, and what is she actually coming to this offer for?

*Why it is a loop:* She is roughly a fifth of the spend, she pays more per lead, and she is the most attentive audience in the account. If her situation is different from the young partnered mom's, and the deeper watch time suggests it is, she needs her own message rather than a harder version of the same one, and nobody has written it.

*Territory: Personas.*

**3. The buyer with no plan at all.**

The account made a video for the uninsured mom, ending on "If you feel like you've fallen through the cracks, this is the bridge." It received $67.51. A static on the same theme ran a $15.07 CPL, better than the account average, on $1,009.79, and was paused. Combined lifetime spend on this audience is about 0.14% of the account.

*Pull: Gap.* The brand named this buyer as one of its five ICPs, wrote real creative for her, and then never gave the creative enough budget for Meta to learn anything.

*Question:* How much demand sits with the mom who has no employer plan at all?

*Why it is a loop:* Every winning script in the account assumes she already has insurance she is overpaying for. If the uninsured mom is a real and reachable segment, the account's entire message architecture excludes her by construction, and that is a whole lane nobody has priced.

*Territory: Personas.*

**4. The children who are never on screen.**

Across 13 ads read in full, the children shown are infants and toddlers with almost no exception, and no teenager appears in any of them. Meanwhile 42.6% of spend delivers to women 35-44 and 12.7% to 45-54. The brand's own stated testing priority is "All different ages of moms with ages of kids."

*Pull: Gap.* There is a large delivered audience whose kids are well past the toddler stage, and nothing on screen reflects their life.

*Question:* Where are the moms of school-age and teenage children in this account's response?

*Why it is a loop:* Casting the child is one of the cheapest variables in the whole production, and if the on-screen child is quietly filtering the audience, the brand is capping its reach with a wardrobe decision rather than a strategic one.

*Territory: Creators and talent.*

**5. The creators who were cast and never funded.**

An ad led by a Black creator got $206.08. One led by an Asian-American creator got $46.83. One led by a woman in her mid-fifties got $34.21. Combined, $287.12. Yet the account's best cost per link click, $0.93, and best CTR, 3.15%, both belong to `moms-53 3`, led by a woman who reads as mixed race or Hispanic.

*Pull: Gap.* The creative was made and then starved, so the roster exists on paper and nothing was ever learned from it.

*Question:* Who does this brand reach when the woman on camera is not white?

*Why it is a loop:* The one non-white creator who did get meaningful spend produced the best click economics in the account. That is one data point and it could be the script rather than the creator, but it is enough to make the untested roster worth a real budget instead of a rounding error.

*Territory: Creators and talent.*

**6. Which half of the winning ad is doing the work.**

`moms-63 2b` and `Moms43 - 4 - V4` carry the identical text hook, the identical offer and the identical state list. Their CPLs are $13.29 and $30.43. The only material difference is the opening visual, an aspirational walk out of a beautiful home versus a mother lying in bed with a baby. Meanwhile the brand describes its winner as "the state angle," which is the part that does not vary.

*Pull: Tension.* The brand's account of why it wins and the account's own variance cannot both be the whole story, because the named winner is the constant and the difference lives in the variable.

*Question:* Which part of the winning ad is actually carrying it, the state gate or the opening frame that wraps it?

*Why it is a loop:* Iteration budget follows this answer. If it is the opener, the team should be making twenty first-ten-seconds against one script. If it is the gate, the openers barely matter and the money should go to reach.

*Territory: Messaging.*

**7. What a mom responds to when there is no state list.**

The Approved State List appears in all 13 ads read at full media depth and behind roughly 92.7% of the 90-day spend. I could not find a funded ad in the current window that asks for the click on any other basis.

*Pull: Gap.* An entire account has been built on one mechanism, and there is no evidence anywhere in the spend about what happens without it.

*Question:* What makes a mom click when the state list is not the reason?

*Why it is a loop:* The gate is a real asset and also a single point of failure. It is a compliance qualifier, a scarcity device and a curiosity hook at once, and if it fatigues or the state footprint changes, the account has no second mechanism and no idea what one would look like.

*Territory: Messaging.*

**8. Which leads actually pick up the phone. For the brand to answer.**

The account produced 4,336 leads in 90 days. The custom conversion built to track a booked consultation, `invitee_meeting_scheduled`, returned no data at all in the window, and only 42 `Call` events fired. Every CPL in this doc is the cost of a phone number, not the cost of a customer.

*Pull: Tension.* The account ranks its creative confidently on CPL while the one number that would say whether a cheap lead is a good lead is missing, and those two things cannot both be trusted.

*Question:* Which of the account's messages produce leads that actually book a call with an agent?

*Why it is a loop:* Only the brand and its partner agencies can answer this, because the outcome lives in their systems. If the aspirational spousal-win ad produces a curious browser and the grievance ad produces a woman in real pain who answers the phone, then the account is scaling the wrong creative at 67.8% of budget and the ranking in this doc inverts. Nothing else in the persona system matters as much as this one answer.

*Territory: Product.* **Routed to the brand.**
