# Strategic roadmap — Health For Moms

## Diagnosis

**This account has spent twenty-one months and $743,218.09 talking to a woman who does not exist, in a voice it cannot legally keep using, and its cheapest proven answer is sitting switched off in its own library.** The woman it addresses is uninsured and wants to feel rescued. The woman it reaches is insured, broke, and does not know she is allowed to leave the plan her job picked for her.

That is the call in two sentences, and here is the discipline behind it. `persona-research-and-creative-strategy-process.md` says a diagnosis has to disqualify ideas, and this one does. It kills the peaceful outcome montage. It kills the flat savings number. It kills any brief that treats the viewer as somebody who has never had coverage. And it greenlights a narrow set of work: change the premise, not the audience.

**The most important thing in this document is that the four strategy inputs converge, and they converged independently.** Each one was run against its own territory's evidence. None was told what the others concluded. All four came back pointing the same way.

- **Persona** says lead against `pays-and-still-owes`, the woman who pays every month and still owes everything, and change what the account says to her rather than who it looks for. The word "deductible" appears in **207 of 1,342** comments and "uninsured" in **2**, both re-pinned live today.
- **Product** says lead with the employer-plan switch, carried as a static, pointed at `www.healthformoms.co/save/`.
- **Messaging** says stop leading with savings and lead with standing. The plainest version is that she is allowed to leave the plan she thinks she is stuck in.
- **Casting** says the woman who already delivers the pitch in both of the account's biggest ads should open them, at 0:03 instead of 0:14 or 0:18.

Four reads, four different bodies of evidence, one answer: **lead with the switch, not the saving.** `creative-strategy-fundamentals.md` treats the cross-read between what the brand believes, what the customer does and what the field rewards as the place the message lives. Here all three of those, plus the account's own delivery data, land in the same spot. That convergence is worth more than any single input, because four instruments agreeing is much harder to explain away than one instrument being loud.

**And the evidence is already bought and paid for.** The two statics that said the switching thing returned **3,815 leads at $11.79 across $44,972.40**, converting **19.04% of their link clicks into leads** against video's **8.28%**, verified live today by a lifetime pull grouped by name. Both are dark. This is not a new idea that needs proving. It is a proven idea that needs restarting, and `persona-research-and-creative-strategy-process.md` is blunt that strong strategy usually looks like redeploying what already worked rather than inventing something new.

### The caveat that sits on every line below, stated once

**Every recommendation in this roadmap clears gate one only.**

The team's own definition of a winner has two gates, in their words: *"CPL is important to scale but then we look at the lead quality as well before really scaling."* Gate one is cost per lead, and Parker can see it on every ad. Gate two is lead quality, and it lives with the partner insurance agencies where no tool in this brain reaches.

Re-pinned live today: the account fired **44 `Call` events across 16 ads against 4,421 leads** in the trailing 90 days, and the `invitee_meeting_scheduled` pixel is defined but returns no data at all. That is roughly one visible downstream event for every hundred leads. The roll-up graded this the highest-stakes loop in the entire brain, at Stakes 5, and ten separate documents arrived at it independently without seeing each other.

So read every number below as the cost of a phone number, never the cost of a customer. Nothing here is called a winner. If the agencies come back and say the cheap leads enroll worse than the expensive ones, the ranking below can invert, and it should. That answer is worth more than everything else in this document combined, and it costs one conversation.

## Top three priorities, in order

Ordered by confidence first and speed second, which is the rule in `persona-research-and-creative-strategy-process.md`. No dates. The user is approving a direction and a sequence, not a schedule.

### Priority 1 — Fix the two claims. One edit, essentially the whole account.

**What it is.** Two claims run on almost every dollar this account spends, both against the brand's own written rules, and both sit inside one shared block of body copy. Repair that block before any new creative ships, because everything new will inherit it.

**This is not a creative decision and it should not be argued about like one.** It is the cheapest high-value action available, it needs no shoot, no casting and no strategy, and its downside is the only asymmetric risk in this document.

**The evidence, and it is the sharpest thing I pulled today.**

A live keyword sweep for the exact string *"saving you 20% or more"* over the trailing 90 days returns **127 of the account's 128 ad-name groups, carrying $100,066.79 of the window's $100,067.42** [`verified live`, 2026-09-04, window 2026-06-06 to 2026-09-03]. That is 99.999% of the money.

A second live sweep for the headline *"Approved by thousands of Mom's across America"* returns **the identical 127 groups and the identical $100,066.79** [`verified live`, 2026-09-04].

**Two different claims, two separate sweeps, the exact same group count and the exact same dollar to the cent.** That is the direct proof that they travel together in one block rather than a coincidence, and it is what makes this a single edit rather than a hundred. Read every one of the ten highest-spending ad names returned today and the shape is visible: the `ad_title` field carries the approval claim and the `ad_content` field carries the savings claim, on `Moms43 - 4 - V3`, `Moms43 - 5 - V1`, `moms-63 2b`, `moms-63 3e`, `moms-53 3` and the rest, word for word, including on the statics.

**One reconciliation, because the number moved.** `messaging-strategy-input.md` recorded the approval headline at **$83,918.11, or 83.9% of window spend**, measured across the eight highest-spending ad names. My sweep today measured the phrase across every group that carries it and returns $100,066.79. Both are honest reads of different scopes. The wider one is the one to carry forward, and it makes the exposure larger than the vault had it, not smaller.

**Why the claims break the rules.** The brand's own guidelines, in a section it heads "THIS SECTION IS NON-NEGOTIABLE," say *"Savings claims: always 'up to 30%' — never drop the 'up to'"* [`stated`, `brand-identity-analysis.md`]. The body copy drops it on essentially every ad. Nothing anywhere in this brain says what the 20%, the 24% or the 30% compares against. On the approval claim: zero customer reviews, zero press, no Better Business Bureau profile for the operating entity, and two pieces of praise in 1,342 comments, both from women who were told no [`verified`, `reputation-analysis.md`, 2026-09-04].

**Why it is urgent rather than tidy.** Two variants of `B1 - Copy 7` already carry a Meta status of DISAPPROVED, holding $8,553.74 and $5,048.37 [`verified live`, 2026-09-04, carried in the messaging input]. And `category-and-market-research.md` documents **six enforcement events between 2024 and April 2026**, four of them federal, all aimed at the marketing layer rather than at carriers: an FTC suit in April 2026 over roughly $91 million alleging carrier and government impersonation, warnings to 21 health plan marketers and lead generators in December 2024 citing prior judgments of $195 million and $100 million, and the Benefytt case, which named **deceptive lead-generation websites** as part of the violation. That last one puts the funnel in scope, not just the plan at the end of it.

The strategic half of this is the useful half. When regulators publicly define what bad looks like, a brand that is visibly and checkably not that has something real to stand on. Every credibility marker this brand carries today is its own assertion.

**Persona and product served.** All of them. This is infrastructure under every priority below.

**Confidence: strong,** and it is the only strong call in this document. The claim text is `verified live`. The rule it breaks is `stated` in the brand's own words. The enforcement record is `verified` from public actions. Nothing about this rests on a persona read or an unclosed loop.

**What it costs, honestly.** Nobody has priced the repair. Every cheap lead this account ever bought came with a flat number attached, and that tension is carried as open loop 1 below. The reason it does not change the ranking: the two disapproved variants mean the asset cannot be reused as written whatever the answer is, and a rule the brand itself marked non-negotiable is not a thing Parker gets to trade against a cost per lead.

### Priority 2 — Lead with the switch, not the saving. Restart the employer-plan message as a static, pointed at `www./save/`.

**What it is.** Move the top of the funnel off the savings number and onto standing: she can decline the plan her job offers and go buy her own. Carry it in the format that converts, aimed at the destination that works.

This is the convergence priority. All four inputs land here.

**The evidence, in four layers.**

**One, who the account already reaches.** Lifetime, re-verified today by summing the static and video pulls: **$743,218.09, 39,569 leads, $18.78 cost per lead**, with **$572,045.85, or 77.0%, delivered to women aged 25 to 44** and 21.2% to women 45 and over. The trailing 90 days tightens it to **81.2% aged 25 to 44 and 95.3% female** [`verified live`, 2026-09-04]. The served audience and the delivered audience have agreed for the account's whole life. This priority does not touch targeting at all.

**Two, what she actually says.** "Deductible" appears in **207 of 1,342** comments and "uninsured" in **2**, both counted live today. She is not a gap in the market. She is somebody else's customer and she is angry about it. The most durable signal in the whole corpus is the employer family, 24 comments naming an employer, which keeps arriving from March through August while nearly everything else went quiet after May. From 2026-08-15, pulled live again today: *"Has anyone tried this? I pay quite a bit for an employee plan with a very high deductible. Is this worth it and is it widely accepted?"*

And then the moment that decides it. On 2026-05-05, under `MOMS38 - 1 - V2`, one commenter answered another: *"And if it's an employer insurance, you don't have to sign up with that employer insurance you can decline the coverage and go out and find your own insurance carrier."* The brand has never said that sentence in an ad. A stranger said it for free, under an ad the brand paid for, and in twenty months and 1,342 comments the brand has never replied once.

**Three, the format, and this is the load-bearing part.** Verified live today on two lifetime pulls:

| Modality | Groups | Spend | Link clicks | Leads | Leads per link click | CPL | CPM | CTR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Static** | 308 | $168,907.73 | 82,871 | 13,735 | **16.57%** | **$12.30** | $17.99 | 1.76% |
| **Video** | 1,259 | $574,310.36 | 312,052 | 25,834 | **8.28%** | $22.23 | $23.68 | 2.28% |

A static's click turns into a lead at exactly twice the rate of a video's. Both denominators are in the tens of thousands of clicks. And the direction of the click-through rate is what makes this a real finding rather than a flattering one: **video wins the click by 30% and then loses the lead by half.** If statics simply bought cheaper junk, their click rate would be higher and their conversion lower. It is the reverse. The account has been optimising toward the thing that wins clicks and away from the thing that wins form fills, and because cost per lead barely moves in this account no alarm ever went off.

`ad-account-analysis.md` warns that statics skew toward the bottom of the funnel and should not be judged against top-of-funnel video on a level field. That caveat is real and it survives here, because 13,735 leads is not a small ad picking off easy conversions and a switching story is top-of-funnel work in a static wrapper. The gap narrows under the caveat. It stays the largest one in the account.

**Four, the lane itself.** Verified live today, grouped by name:

| Ad | Spend | Link clicks | Leads | Leads per link click | CPL | CPM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `B1 - Copy 7` | $30,339.32 | 11,845 | 2,584 | 21.82% | $11.74 | $15.89 |
| `B1 samar- Copy 1` | $14,633.08 | 8,193 | 1,231 | 15.03% | $11.89 | $15.05 |
| **Together** | **$44,972.40** | **20,038** | **3,815** | **19.04%** | **$11.79** | |

Against the brand's own scoreboard, where under $15 is "fantastic," $11.79 is 21% below that ceiling. The account has been in the "meh" band for six straight months. And those are the cheapest impressions in the whole library, so this lane was not buying scraps. It was buying cheap attention that converted.

**What the message has to sound like, and this is the messaging input's call.** Calm permission, not distress. The account's own money says so. Over the 30 days to 2026-09-02 the calm openers delivered leads at **$13.29 to $24.45** while the high-distress crying openers delivered at **$29.85 to $38.09**, blending to $33.23 across six ads, while posting the *higher* hook rates at 57.02% and 52.40% [`verified`, `monthly-hook-audit.md`]. They stopped more people and cost half again as much. The account is currently building more of the distress lane, six ads and climbing. On its own numbers that production is buying the wrong thing.

**And the proof has to be a person, not a percentage.** `killer-performance-ads.md` puts promise and proof at rules two and three together, and this account has the promise without the proof. The audience corrects the ad's central term **103 times across 13 ads**, some of them identifying themselves as licensed brokers and one as a medical biller of thirty years. In this category, using the words right *is* a credential. The brand owns a compliant authority it has never filmed: a real licensed partner advisor already inside its own funnel. Across 147 delivering ads nobody credentialed has ever spoken.

**The destination, and the one live fault to clear.** Point the money at `www.healthformoms.co/save/`. As of a live lookup today, **all four `go.healthformoms.co/save/` ads are still ACTIVE**, together holding **$3,474.43, 2,188 link clicks and 18 leads**, which is 0.82% leads per link click. One of them, `moms-53 3 - Copy`, has taken **511 link clicks and produced zero leads** [`verified live`, 2026-09-04]. The roll-up routed this urgent on 2026-09-04 and it is still running.

**Do not overclaim the `go.` verdict in either direction.** The validation came back **inconclusive on cause**, and it earned that verdict honestly. A healthy March cohort on the same subdomain converted at **8.69% leads per link click**, indistinguishable from `www.`'s 9.50% to 10.63%, and its landing-page-view collapse is a proven counting artifact. The August cohort collapsed to 0.82%. Campaign, creation date and destination are perfectly collinear in the failing cell and no cut of this account separates them. The subdomain is not proven broken. What is not in doubt is what to do: those four ads are duplicates of creative that works fine on `www.`, so switching them off costs the brand nothing it is not already getting.

**Persona and product served.** `pays-and-still-owes`, the flagship, carrying the `employer-plan-locked` state. The product is the same one the brand always sells; what changes is which door she walks through.

**Confidence: mixed, and the parts are not equal.** Gate one is `verified` at real scale on two independent ratios. The read that this buyer is also the one most likely to survive the underwriting gate is `inferred`, not verified, and it rests on the shape of who employer coverage selects for rather than on any enrollment data. It is worth saying why that inference matters anyway: it is the one place in this account where gate one and gate two plausibly point the same way instead of opposite ways. Every other efficiency finding here carries the worry that the cheapest lead is the least qualifiable one. This one carries it less.

**What it costs.** It moves money away from the hook carrying two thirds of the account. It restarts a production lane the team switched off around March for a reason nobody wrote down, which is the single question that most directly gates this call. And it aims at the buyer whose existing deal is hardest to beat, because employer premiums come out of payroll before tax. The one complete customer outcome in 1,342 comments is a woman who was quoted $400 a month under an ad promising a $400 saving. That does not make the lane wrong. It makes it unproven in the one place it could have been proven.

### Priority 3 — Open the ads with the woman who already closes them.

**What it is.** The account's two biggest ads both hand the pitch to the same woman, described by Parker's own read of the media as a mature woman in her late forties or early fifties in a green floral dress. She has never once opened an ad. Move her to frame zero.

**The comparison the whole call rests on**, re-verified live today with full creative fields on both ads:

| | `Moms43 - 4 - V3` | `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` |
|---|---|---|
| Lifetime spend | $57,506.21 | $54,173.20 |
| Leads | 2,668 | 2,689 |
| Cost per lead | $21.55 | **$20.15** |
| Hook rate | 45.84% | 32.34% |
| **Hold rate** | **3.81%** | **17.12%** |
| Share of spend to women 45+ | 10.6% (POV lifetime) | **24.8%** |
| The older woman arrives at | 0:18 | 0:14 |
| Status today | ACTIVE | ADSET_PAUSED |

Same brand, same offer, same state list, same kind of woman delivering the pitch. Two ads within $3,300 of each other in spend and within 21 leads of each other in volume. **The one that reaches her four seconds sooner holds four and a half times as many viewers, buys a cheaper lead, and reaches more than twice as many women over 45.** And the one that holds 17.12% is the one that is switched off.

Hold that against the bar in `ad-account-analysis.md`. Hook floor is 30% and 45 to 50% is strong, so the flagship stops the scroll beautifully. Hold floor is 12 to 15%, and at 3.81% the account's biggest spender misses it by a factor of three while the paused ad clears it comfortably. `killer-performance-ads.md` explains the failure precisely: seconds one to three owe the viewer a reason to keep watching and seconds three to five owe her the best benefit or a re-hook. A silent montage with an acoustic track and no spoken word until 0:18 gives her neither. The account's biggest ad wins the thumb and then has nothing to say to the person it just stopped.

**Why this is third and not second.** It is a casting and edit move, not a premise move, so it serves Priority 2 rather than replacing it. And its weakest link is honest: the cost-per-lead gap between those two ads is **$1.40, about 6.5%**, on two ads that differ in more than one way. The hold gap is four and a half times and much harder to explain away. The cost gap is small.

**Why it is third and not lower.** It is the cheapest real thing on this list to produce. The brand names **casting and creator sourcing as its number one creative bottleneck** and production as its fourth [`stated`, brand context]. This recommendation does not fight that bottleneck at all. The woman is already cast, already filmed, already in the two biggest ads in the account. What changes is the edit order. In the tier language of `persona-research-and-creative-strategy-process.md` this is **Tier 1, an edit or a re-cut**, the highest-confidence and fastest-to-ship kind of work there is. With Open Enrollment 58 days out and the brand's own two-month planning deadline already passed, a Tier 1 re-cut is one of the very few casting moves that can still land inside the window.

**The second half of the call: put a second person in the frame.** Every configuration in this account that puts a second character in frame or on the line clears or nearly clears the 12% hold floor. Every configuration that leaves one woman alone with her phone sits under 6%. The two-woman podcast conversation `MOMS25 BLAST - X1 - Copy 2` bought **175 leads at $19.90 on $3,483.03 with a 10.29% hold**, and has not been repeated in ten months. The one-actor two-role phone call runs its two best builds at **$22.87 and $22.50**, at or below the window's own $22.63, while holding **27.31% and 25.36%**.

**Persona and product served.** It serves `pays-and-still-owes` first, because that is who these two ads deliver to. It is also the cheapest way to buy some of the whitespace around `already-knows`, the woman 45 and over, without handing her the lead slot she cannot afford.

**Three things it rules out, and they matter as much as the thing it rules in.** No lab coat as the speaker, because the account already ran that experiment and the clinical setting delivered **52.7% of its spend to women 25 to 34** and 3.8% to women 45 to 54, the youngest delivery of anything inspected. No synthetic presenter, which is the closest thing to a rule in this brand's entire customer record, six rejections across three separate years and four ad families. No husband on camera yet, because the literal string "wife of the year" returns **0 of 1,342** comments, re-checked live today: nobody repeats the line and nobody rejects it, so the idea rests on a naming pattern rather than a customer signal.

**Confidence: moderate.** That she already delivers the pitch in both giants is `verified` from `creator_demographic` and `ad_summary` on both ads. That casting acts as an age lever reproduces three independent ways. That leading with her lowers cost per lead is `inferred` from a small gap between two ads that differ in more than one variable. And one thing genuinely undercuts it, named rather than buried: **hold rate and cost per lead run backwards in this account.** The three formats with the strongest hold are among the most expensive per lead, at $32.00, $31.82 and $33.62 against the account's $22.64, all on under $2,100 of spend. That is open loop 2 below, and it is the loop that decides whether this priority is a lead-cost move or only an attention move.

## Roads not taken

Eight directions were genuinely on the table. Each was set aside for a specific reason, so the user can approve with their eyes open.

**1. Lead with `already-knows`, the woman 45 and over.** This is the runner-up and it is a real one. Her case is the strongest single instrument in the persona system, because the age split comes from Meta delivery rather than from comments, and it reproduces two independent ways. She holds attention where the flagship's creative does not. Nobody has ever built an ad for her on purpose. Three things hold her out of the lead slot: she is about a fifth of delivery and falling, from 21.2% lifetime to 16.2% in the trailing quarter; the creative that reaches her costs **$26.58 per lead against the account's $18.78 lifetime blend, 41.5% more**; and her supporting corpus is 16 comments, which is a candidate rather than a pattern. The team's stated objective is efficiency before volume. A funded bet is defensible on that objective. A lead call is not, while the gate that would justify the premium is invisible. **She gets the casting work in Priority 3 and a sized bet, not the lead.**

**2. Lead with pregnancy and new-baby demand.** On demand alone this would be first: 38 of 1,342 comments, fourteen months, the highest like counts in the corpus, **63.2% of the category's organic feed**, and a lifetime keyword search for "pregnant" across every ad field returning zero. In a normal audit that is textbook whitespace. It is not whitespace here. The plans are medically underwritten, the funnel screens her out, and the team confirmed on 2026-09-04 that it deliberately removed pregnancy from its own inspiration set. Leading here means paying to create a public complaint the brand cannot resolve, under an ad it bought, with its own name as the punchline. **This is a recruitment cost to reduce, not a swing to take.**

**3. Lead into the chronic-condition family.** Rejected for the same reason with more force. The most emotionally intense identity in the corpus, and medical underwriting screens her out. Reaching her harder makes the problem worse.

**4. Keep leading with savings and "wife of the year."** Rejected as the lead, **kept running**. This matters and it is not a hedge. `seasonality.md` is explicit that evergreen winners stay on while seasonal and new creative layers in, and only get displaced when the data says so. The POV family carries **$87,991.09 across 20 ads at a $19.56 blended cost per lead** with no measured decay across twenty uses. It works on gate one. It is not the growth call because it rests on a phrase with zero uptake in 1,342 comments, invites a bidding contest rather than a conversation, and carries the claim Priority 1 has to repair. **Do not swap it out. Build beside it.**

**5. Lead with the income gap, the mother who earns too much for help and too little to pay.** Rejected on a hard constraint rather than on evidence. Her whole story is a comparison between two public programs the brand's own non-negotiable rules forbid naming, and commenters name those two 17 and 14 times respectively. Write her story without those two nouns and the income figure, the cliff and the plan that costs more than the house note all survive. Write it with them and it is sharper and against the rules. **That reconciliation is the brand's to make, not Parker's**, and it is a brand-routed question in the roll-up.

**6. Put the Approved State List in the first frame across the account.** The product input's runner-up, and something specific demoted it. `moms-53 3` holds the account's best click-through rate at **3.15%** and its cheapest link click at **$0.96**, both best in the file. Then run it on the trustworthy ratio: over the 90 days it took **2,127 link clicks and produced 127 leads, 5.97% leads per link click**, against `moms-63 2b`'s 12.25% in the same window. **The state list buys the cheapest clicks in the account and converts them worst at scale.** The likely reading, and it is `inferred`, is that a full screen of state names makes people stop and hunt, and the ones who do not find their state click anyway and get turned away. It stays a strong qualifying device inside an ad. It is not the lead.

**7. Push the quiz funnel as the new destination.** Rejected for now on evidence rather than principle. `quiz.healthformoms.com` holds **$138.27 lifetime, 57 link clicks and 2 leads**, and it grew from 26 ads to 30 with a second path in the 24 hours before the product input was written. Its landing-page-view counter reports normal scores of 88.89 and 100, so unlike `go.` it is instrumented. The position is not kill it. It is keep it small until it has a lead count worth reading, and **do not let it near the November budget on the strength of a hunch**.

**8. Ramp hard into December the way the account ramped for Open Enrollment 2025.** Deliberately not recommended, and this is the seasonal call. The 2025 ramp worked on volume and was efficiency-neutral: November held cost per lead nearly flat at **$13.08** on the year's biggest lead month, because click-through rate rose 41.3% and paid for a 31.6% rise in impression cost. Then December broke the trade. It is the most expensive month of all thirteen readable months at a **$29.34 CPM**, with cost per lead up **39.8% to $18.28** and the share of landing page views becoming leads falling to 14.2%, on largely the same creative and the same funnel. `seasonality.md` says the useful question inside a season is never whether demand spikes but what shifts underneath it. Something about the buyer changes between those two months and nobody knows what. **Layer for November. Do not buy December on the assumption that it is November's second half**, and note that the larger structural event is not Open Enrollment at all: the enhanced ACA subsidies expire **2026-01-01**, and 47.4% of the category's relevant organic posts land October to December.

## Open loops

Four loops, and each one is here for the same reason: it could change the ranking above rather than the execution below it.

**1. Every cheap lead this brand has ever bought came with a claim the brand says it cannot make.**

The two cheapest lead sources in the account's history are both statics carrying flat savings claims: `B1 samar- Copy` at $54,224.86 and 4,441 leads at $12.21 on the "saving you 20% or more" body copy, and `B1 - Copy 7` at $30,339.32 and 2,584 leads at $11.74 on a flat "24% cheaper" headline, with two of its five variants sitting at DISAPPROVED. The account's compliant live video delivers at $22.23 lifetime.

*Pull: Tension.* It fired because two things this brain treats as settled cannot both be right: the brand's own hard rule says the flat claim is forbidden, and the flat claim is on every ad that ever bought a cheap lead.

*Question: What is actually doing the work in this account's cheapest ads?*

*Why it matters:* it prices Priority 1 and sizes Priority 2. If the flat number is what buys the cheap lead, the repair has a cost nobody has calculated and the team should know it before paying it. If the work is being done by the format, the switching story or the single designed frame, then the claim gets repaired for free and the lane restarts at full strength.

*Territory: Messaging.*

**2. In this account, the ads that hold attention cost more per lead, not less.**

The three formats with the strongest hold rates are among the most expensive per lead, at $32.00, $31.82 and $33.62 against the account's $22.64. Meanwhile `moms-53 3` holds 3.82% and buys the best click economics in the file. All three of the high-hold formats sit under $2,100 of lifetime spend, so a handful of leads swings each number.

*Pull: Surprise.* It fired because the relationship every craft doc assumes, that deeper attention converts better, is running backwards on this account's own money.

*Question: What is happening between the stop and the form fill that makes an ad that holds attention cost more per lead?*

*Why it matters:* it decides whether Priority 3 is a cost move or only an attention move. If hold does not predict cost here, the casting re-cut is still right for reach and entity diversity but should not be sold to the team as a way to buy cheaper leads, and it drops behind work that does.

*Territory: Messaging.*

**3. The season this plan is built for may not be the season that matters.**

Open Enrollment opens 2026-11-01 and the brand names it the most important date on its calendar. The account says something else: November 2025 took 5.4% of spend while March through May 2026 took 58.9%, and December is the worst month in the whole readable series. Meanwhile the enhanced ACA subsidies expire 2026-01-01, an event that moved roughly 4.8 million people out of coverage they could previously afford, and the account has spent $379.92 in its entire life on creative that names a subsidy.

*Pull: Gap.* It fired because the single largest thing happening to this brand's buyer in the next four months has almost no money and no creative behind it, while the date everyone is planning around is the account's fifth-biggest month.

*Question: What changes for this brand's buyer when the enhanced subsidies end on 2026-01-01?*

*Why it matters:* it decides where the ramp points. If the subsidy cliff is the real event, the plan above should be built to land in December and January rather than to peak on November 1, and the sequencing of all three priorities moves with it.

*Territory: Personas.*

**4. The cheapest message this account ever ran went almost entirely to the youngest band it reaches.**

The two switching statics sent **83.4% of their money to women aged 25 to 44 and 15.7% to women 45 and over**, and a live lookup today puts `B1 samar- Copy 1` at 22.1% to the 45-plus band. Every other document in this brain files the employer-plan signal as something that cuts across the flagship and the older woman both.

*Pull: Tension.* The vault says this message belongs to two personas and the delivery says it belongs almost entirely to one, and both cannot be a fair description of who that headline finds.

*Question: Who is the woman that the "you can leave your job's plan" message actually finds?*

*Why it matters:* it decides whether Priority 2 and Priority 3 are one move or two. If the switching message is the flagship's own, the older woman still needs creative built from scratch and her bet costs what the Skit numbers say it costs. If it reaches both, the two priorities collapse into one production run.

*Territory: Personas.*
