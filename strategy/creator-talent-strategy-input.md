# Creator and talent strategy input — Health For Moms

## The call

**Lead with the woman in her late forties or early fifties, and put her in the first three seconds instead of the fourteenth.**

She is not a new hire. She is already in this account, already carrying the two biggest ads it has ever run, and she has never once been allowed to open one. In both of those ads she walks on after a younger woman has held the screen alone, and in both she is the person who explains. The account has cast her as the closer roughly forty times and as the hook exactly zero times.

Here is the comparison the whole call rests on. The account's two largest ads are almost the same size and they cost very different amounts.

| | `Moms43 - 4 - V3` | `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` |
|---|---|---|
| Lifetime spend | $57,506.21 | $54,173.20 |
| Leads | 2,668 | 2,689 |
| Cost per lead | $21.55 | **$20.15** |
| Hook rate | 45.84% | 32.34% |
| **Hold rate** | **3.81%** | **17.12%** |
| Average play time | 4 seconds | 7 seconds |
| Share of spend to women 45+ | 10.6% (POV lifetime) | **24.8%** |
| The older woman arrives at | 0:18 | 0:14 |

*Verified*, Parker MCP lifetime pulls on 2026-09-04, both read with their full creative fields.

Same brand, same offer, same state list, same kind of woman delivering the pitch. The one that reaches her four seconds sooner holds four and a half times as many viewers, plays three seconds longer, buys a cheaper lead, and reaches more than twice as many women over 45. That is not a small edge and it is not a fluke of a thin ad. It is $54,173 against $57,506.

The second half of the call is about how many people are in the room with her. **Put a second person in the frame.** The account has done that four ways and every one of them beat the account average on watch time, including one nobody in this brain had found until today: a two-woman podcast conversation that bought a **$19.90** lead on $3,483.03 and held **10.29%** of viewers.

So the casting direction, in one sentence a producer can act on: **a woman who reads as forty-five to fifty-five, on camera from frame zero, explaining something to a second person the viewer can see or hear, filmed plainly in a real room.**

Three things this call rules out, and they matter as much as the thing it rules in:

- **Do not cast a doctor to speak.** The lab coat has already run and it is the wrong lever, for reasons below.
- **Do not use a synthetic presenter.** This is the closest thing to a rule in this brand's whole customer record.
- **Do not put a husband on camera yet.** It is the loudest absence in the library and there is no evidence under it.

**Confidence: moderate.** The direction rests on real spend and reproduces two independent ways, but every number in it clears gate one only, and the deciding question is an open loop rather than a validated finding. The runner-up direction, a licensed partner advisor on camera, is genuinely stronger on trust and cannot be recommended today because nobody has confirmed a real one can be filmed.

## Why — the talent-persona match

### Who the account casts, read from the media

Read across everything inspected at full media depth, the answer is narrow and consistent. **One woman, alone, in a bright room, talking to her own phone.** That is every pitch segment in the current window, and `visual-vocabulary.md` records it from frame-level reads of three videos plus the `creator_demographic` field across the library. She is described overwhelmingly as a white woman in her late twenties to early thirties, in casual domestic clothes: hoodies, tank tops, tie-dye, messy buns, gold hoops.

The children on screen are infants and toddlers, near enough without exception. Across thirteen ads read at full media depth upstream, **no teenager appears in any of them**. *Verified*, carried from `source-pulls/ad-account.md`.

Men are close to absent. The full list of men Parker can find on screen anywhere in this library: a young street interviewer holding a branded microphone, a masked male doctor in `OMC-Health for Moms-[B1-C9-V1]`, a stitched male creator in a lab coat in `Moms44 - 1 - V3`, one male voiceover on an end card, and two animated men in the claymation. **No husband has ever appeared on camera**, in an account whose single most-repeated sentence is *"POV: Telling your husband you found better health insurance."* That line has run on **20 distinct video ads carrying $87,991.09 and 4,499 leads at a $19.56 blended cost per lead**. *Verified*, two independent keyword pulls in `audits/2026-09/monthly-hook-audit.md`.

### Who the account actually reaches

Lifetime, the account has spent **$743,218.09 across 1,560 ad-name groups for 39,569 leads at an $18.78 cost per lead**. *Verified*, live pull 2026-09-04. Delivery runs **96.1% female, 99.3% mobile, 58.1% Facebook**, and the age split is:

- 25 to 34: 32.5%
- 35 to 44: 44.5%
- **45 to 54: 16.4%**
- 55 to 64: 4.2%
- 65 and over: 0.6%

So **21.2% of every dollar this brand has ever spent reached a woman 45 or older**, and the creative aimed at her is a rounding error. `personas-profile.md` names her `already-knows` and calls her the secondary persona, held at **mixed** confidence like all three, with the honest note that she has **no creative built for her**.

### Where the cast and the buyer come apart

The gap is not that the wrong woman is on screen. It is that the right woman is on screen at the wrong moment, in the wrong role, saying the wrong thing.

`personas-profile.md` describes `already-knows` from the delivery data and from sixteen comments across seven ads on a 1,322 denominator. She has managed this problem for years. She is the most engaged person in the audience by a wide margin. She is asking, politely, whether she still counts as a mom. *"OK, this is great for mom's of young children. What about the rest of us? ... My deductible used to be reasonable, now it's almost $7000 a year"* (2026-04-26, 2 likes). *"What if your kids are over 18, do I still qualify as a Mom?"* (2026-04-29). *"What if I'm a mom, but my son is in college 🤔 i pay more $ to take care of him now than ever before ijs"* (2026-03-28). Nobody answers on the thread.

Now read that against `advertising-to-older-audiences.md`, which is the method doc this whole read runs through. Two of its rules land directly on the casting:

**"Match the testimonial to their age."** The doc is blunt: proof from people their own age lands, and proof from a much younger face does not transfer, because the viewer does not see herself in it. Right now the woman answering her question is twenty-eight and lying on white bedding with an infant. The doc calls that a common misread, not a stylistic preference.

**"Direct callouts pull relevance."** The doc holds that subtlety underperforms with this group and an explicit signal that the ad is for her beats an implied one. A face that reads as her own age is the cheapest direct callout available, and it works with the sound off, which `visuals.md` says is the normal case rather than the worst one.

There is one more thing in the corpus that belongs here, and it is the only record in 1,342 comments that rejects the casting rather than the copy. On **2026-09-03**, the last day of the corpus, under `moms-63 2b` and its copy, a commenter wrote:

> *"I'm sure the mom that can afford matching outfits prob just pays in cash 🤦‍♀️"*

*Stated*, `voc-anti-language.md`, one record, marked **thin** there and marked thin here. One comment cannot carry a casting rule. It earns its place for a precise reason: it names the exact failure the method predicts. The woman on screen visibly does not have the problem the ad says she has. And it lands on `moms-63 2b`, one of the two cheapest leads among the window's top spenders at **$13.26**, which is the uncomfortable version of the finding rather than the tidy one.

## Why — the casting the account and the field have won on

### The finding: the older woman is already winning and nobody noticed, because she arrives late

Both of the account's two biggest ads hand the pitch to the same casting type, and Parker's own read of the media says so in both.

`Moms43 - 4 - V3`, the flagship. Its `creator_demographic` field: *"The primary spokesperson is a mature Caucasian female, likely in her late 40s or early 50s, with shoulder-length blonde hair, wearing a green floral dress. She appears professional, maternal, and trustworthy."* She does not speak until **0:18**. For eighteen seconds the ad is a young mother and an infant lying on white bedding under a block of text, with an acoustic track and nobody talking. *Verified* from a frame-level read carried in `visual-vocabulary.md` and from the live `ad_summary` today.

`MOMS38 - 1 - V8`, the sibling of the account's number-two ad and built from the same footage. Its `creator_demographic`: *"The main creator is a middle-aged white woman, late 40s to early 50s, with blonde, shoulder-length hair and blue eyes. She projects a maternal, calm, and trustworthy authority. She wears a green dress with a small white floral print... The secondary creator (hook) is a white woman in her early 30s with long dark hair... appearing as a relatable, frustrated consumer."* She takes over at **0:14**, after fourteen seconds of a younger woman saying her deductible is six thousand dollars. *Verified*, live pull today.

Four seconds of difference. Here is what those four seconds are worth, and this is the strongest number in the document:

- `Moms43 - 4 - V3`: hold **3.81%**, average play **4 seconds**, cost per lead **$21.55** on $57,506.21.
- `MOMS38 - 1 - V1`: hold **17.12%**, average play **7 seconds**, **52.94% of three-second viewers still there at fifteen seconds**, cost per lead **$20.15** on $54,173.20.
- `MOMS38 - 1 - V8`, the same build at small spend: hold **17.33%**, cost per lead **$21.61** on $777.94.

*Verified*, all three from a direct two-ad lookup and a keyword pull today.

The craft floor for hold rate in `ad-account-analysis.md` is 12% to 15%. The flagship misses it by a factor of three. The grievance ad clears it. And `killer-performance-ads.md` explains why in a sentence: seconds one through three owe the viewer a reason to keep watching and seconds three through five owe her the best benefit or a re-hook. A silent lifestyle montage gives her neither. The account's own biggest ad wins the thumb and then has nothing to say to the person it just stopped.

### The finding reproduces in the delivery data, which is a second instrument

`persona-research-and-creative-strategy-process.md` is firm that a claim gets stronger when two different kinds of source agree. Here the second source is Meta delivery, which reports who was served rather than who typed a comment.

- `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` put **20.7% of its spend on women 45 to 54, 3.3% on 55 to 64 and 0.8% on 65 and over — 24.8% to women 45 and older**, against the account's lifetime 21.2%.
- The lab-coat group `MOMS30 - 1 - V20` went the other way entirely: **52.7% to women 25 to 34** and **3.8% to 45 to 54**.
- The whole street-interview unit `Internal Videos - OCT - Copy 7` put **20.6% on 45 to 54, 11.3% on 55 to 64 and 1.4% on 65 and over — 33.3% to women 45 and older**, and delivered **82.2% on Facebook**.
- The named finance creator in `MOMS34 - 4 - 4b` put **20.3% on 45 to 54, 12.2% on 55 to 64 and 5.8% on 65 and over — 38.3% to women 45 and older**, the oldest delivery of anything inspected.

*Verified*, live demographic breakdowns today.

`personas-profile.md` already found this by format and marked the honest caveat: the 90-day Skit cut showing **34.8%** of spend to 45 to 54 sits on only **$712.20** and is not a sturdy number, while the lifetime cut showing Skit at **23.9%** to 45 to 54 against POV's **8.6%** rests on **$32,935.04** and is. The direction is solid at roughly three times, not the four and a half times the thin cut suggests. My pulls today reproduce the direction a third way, by casting rather than by format tag, and the ads carrying it are large.

Read through `andromeda-v2.md`, this is the one persona change the delivery system actually rewards. That doc puts persona at Tier 3 of the differentiation hierarchy and then adds the condition that decides everything: a persona change **must** come with a visible change in the first three seconds or it creates no new Entity ID and buys no incremental reach. The account's current move is the opposite of that. It runs the same young opener and swaps the woman who arrives at second eighteen, which Meta reads as the same ad. Putting the older woman in frame zero is a Tier 3 change that finally satisfies the Tier 2 condition.

## Why — the casting whitespace

The gap pull fires three times here. Two of them are real lanes and one of them is a trap.

### Whitespace 1: she has never opened an ad. Not once.

This is the lane, and it is narrower and sharper than "cast older women," because the brand already casts older women. It casts them as the person who arrives after the hook.

### Whitespace 2: the roster that was cast and never funded

The two ads whose creator Parker's own read names as Black or Asian-American carry **$252.91 between them, ever**:

## The size of it

`persona-research-and-creative-strategy-process.md` says to size a direction against the alternatives and against the roster before recommending it. Here is that arithmetic.

**How much money the direction can touch.** POV touched **$70,052.94 of the window's $99,266.98, or 70.6%**. The spousal-win line carries **$87,991.09 lifetime across 20 ads**. That is the pool this call is asking to change the front three seconds of. It is not asking to kill it. `iterations.md` doctrine and `andromeda-v2.md` agree on the move: hold the message, change the visible first three seconds, and you get a new Entity ID and fresh reach instead of a piggybacked one.

**What it would cost to produce.** Close to nothing, and this is the strongest practical argument for it. The brand names **"Casting/UGC Creators"** as its number one creative bottleneck and **"Production/Filming"** as number four. *Stated*, brand context Section 11, carried in `audits/2026-Q3/90-day-diversity-audit.md`. This recommendation does not fight that bottleneck. The woman is already cast, already filmed, already in the two biggest ads in the account. What changes is the brief and the edit order, not the booking. In the tier language of the persona method this is **Tier 1, an edit or a re-cut**, the highest-confidence and fastest-to-ship kind of work there is.

The second person in frame is a real production ask and it is smaller than it looks. Ranked cheapest first:

1. **One actor, two roles.** No second person, no second call sheet. One wardrobe change and one angle change. Proven five times.
2. **Two people in a room.** Proven at $3,483.03 with a $19.90 lead. Needs a second person and a location.
3. **Two people in public with a microphone.** Proven eight times at $1,172.43. Needs a location, a permit-free public space, and a shoot day. Most expensive, thinnest evidence, most fragile.

**What it is being weighed against.** The alternative that would beat it on trust is the licensed partner advisor, and it cannot be sized because nobody knows whether one can be filmed. That is roll-up brand-routed question **E1**. The alternative that would beat it on volume is the static library, which is a modality call rather than a casting call and belongs to the product and messaging inputs.

**The gate-two caveat, stated once and applying to every number above.** `running-notes/success-definition.md` records the team's own bar verbatim: *"CPL is important to scale but then we look at the lead quality as well before really scaling."* Every cost-per-lead figure in this document clears gate one. Not one of them says anything about gate two. `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` is a **gate-one candidate at $20.15**, not a winner, and if the older woman brings leads the partner agencies cannot enrol, this entire call is wrong in a way Parker has no instrument to detect.

**The clock.** Open Enrollment starts **2026-11-01**, which is 58 days out, and the roll-up records that this team plans creative roughly two months ahead, so the planning deadline for that window has already passed. A Tier 1 re-cut is one of the very few casting moves that can still land inside it.

## Alternatives considered and why not

### The runner-up: a licensed partner advisor on camera

This is the direction that would beat the lead call if one question were answered, and it is the strongest recommendation in three separate audits.

The case for it is genuinely strong. `advertising-to-older-audiences.md` names authority as the trust shortcut for this demographic and puts it bluntly: *"the news are their influencers."* `reputation-analysis.md` establishes that trust, not attention, is this brand's binding constraint. Search the brand plus "legit" and the FBI's **Emerging Discount Medical Insurance Scams** page and the FTC's **Spot Health Insurance Scams** page come back ahead of the brand's own site. The FBI's own advice is to verify a provider with the state insurance department or the Better Business Bureau, and a search for Insurance Yeti LLC in Orlando returns no Better Business Bureau profile at all. *Verified*, live searches 2026-09-04. Meanwhile the brand's own customers ran the test and reported back: *"How come when I search for this on Google, nothing comes up?"* (2025-08-07). *"A Google search doesn't pull anything about this. Is it legit?"* (2025-07-24).

And the brand owns a compliant authority already. Its rules ban a fake doctor character and permit a real licensed professional. Its whole business model routes to licensed advisors at partner agencies. `reputation-analysis.md` lists the authority assets the brand could build and has none of, and **"a licensed agent on camera"** is on that list.

Why it is the runner-up and not the call: **nobody has confirmed a partner advisor can be filmed, or what compliance would let that person say.** That is question E1 in the roll-up's brand-routed set, and it is routed there precisely because no tool Parker holds can answer it. `persona-research-and-creative-strategy-process.md` is clear that confidence comes before speed. Recommending a shoot that may be legally or contractually impossible is not a strategy, it is a wish.

**What would settle it:** one conversation. If a licensed partner advisor can appear and speak, this direction moves ahead of the lead call for the trust-building half of the account, and the two combine well, because an advisor in her forties or fifties satisfies both at once.

### Casting a doctor, or anyone in a lab coat, as the speaker

**She never speaks.** The entire spoken content of the ad is one line at 0:11 from an unnamed narrator. **And it points the wrong way demographically.** The lab coat delivered **52.7% of its spend to women 25 to 34 and 3.8% to women 45 to 54**, and ran **62.2% on Instagram** against the account's 58.1% Facebook. *Verified* today. Then the compliance wall, which is where a casting recommendation stops being a recommendation. The category's own biggest video makes the distinction plain. Hendry's 6.2 million views come from a **real** doctor doing real work with her own clinic's logo in frame. The mechanic is not the coat. It is that she is doing the thing patients are told they cannot do, live, on the phone. A hired actor in a coat borrows the picture and none of the mechanism.

## Confidence and what would raise it

**Overall confidence in the lead call: moderate.** Here is the honest breakdown, because the parts are not equally solid.

**Strong.** That the older woman is already delivering the pitch in the account's two biggest ads, and arrives at 0:18 and 0:14. That is read from `creator_demographic` and `ad_summary` on both ads, live today, plus a frame-level read of one of them. There is no ambiguity in it.

**Strong.** That casting acts as an age lever. This reproduces three independent ways: by opener type across nine per-ad breakdowns upstream, by format tag in the persona work's own live pull, and by ad-level casting in my pulls today. It rests on $32,935.04 of lifetime Skit spend and $54,173.20 on `MOMS38 - 1 - V1`, not on the thin $712.20 window cut.

**Moderate.** That leading with her would lower cost per lead. The evidence is a **$1.40 gap, about 6.5%**, between two ads that differ in more than one way. `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` also has a different opener, a different message and a grievance register. I cannot isolate the casting from the script, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The hold-rate gap is four and a half times and much harder to explain away; the cost gap is small.

**Weak, and it undercuts the whole thing.** That hold rate predicts cost per lead in this account **at all**. **Absent.** Gate two. Nothing in this document knows whether any of these leads are good.

**Absent.** Any rival casting read at all.

### The one thing that would raise confidence most

Not a bigger analysis. A funded test that changes one variable.

The account already holds the perfect control. `Moms43 - 4 - V3` and `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` carry nearly identical spend and nearly identical lead volume, and they are the two largest ads in the brand's history. The clean question is what happens to cost per lead and to hold when the **only** thing that changes is who is in frame zero, with the same words, the same state list, the same room. That is a Tier 1 re-cut of an asset that already exists, and per `andromeda-v2.md` it clears the Entity ID bar on its own, because a different person in the opening frame is exactly the visible first-three-seconds change the system rewards.

Two more things would move it, in order of how much they would change the call:

**Answer E1.** Can a licensed partner advisor be filmed, and what would compliance let her say? One conversation. If the answer is yes, the runner-up becomes the lead and the two directions merge, because an advisor in her late forties is both at once.

**Get an independent read of how mothers in this category talk.** That is Tier 1 loop 3 in the roll-up. Every persona in this brain is capped at **mixed** for one structural reason: 1,342 comments under the brand's own paid ads is the only first-party language surface that exists, and 60.1% of it sits on one creative family. `already-knows` is the persona this casting call serves and she is nearly silent in that corpus, sixteen comments, against a body of viewers absorbing 30% to 38% of grievance-ad spend. Either she watches and does not type, which the older-audience method would predict, or the corpus simply cannot see her. Judging her size from comment volume alone would badly undercount her, and right now that is the only instrument available.

### How to brief it, since the brief is where a casting call lives or dies

`creator-briefs.md` makes one call that decides most briefs: **authorities do better with more freedom.** A credible expert or a strong personality performs worse on a tight script and better with room to talk in her own voice. The more the ad rides on who the person is, the looser the brief should be.

That applies squarely here. The woman this call recommends is being cast for what she has lived through, so a director's brief locked to the beat would waste her. The right split is **direction on structure, freedom on delivery**: hand her the arc and let her find the words. And lead with references rather than description, because a reference video carries tone, pacing and energy in a way a paragraph cannot. The brand owns two good ones already, `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` for the register and `MOMS25 BLAST - X1 - Copy 2` for the two-person shape.

Two production notes that follow from the method rather than from taste. `advertising-to-older-audiences.md` asks for slower edits, longer holds, big high-contrast type and one clear thing on screen at a time, and warns that legibility failures sink otherwise-fine ads. `hooks.md` adds a nuance that cuts against it and should be held alongside rather than ignored: older audiences are not an exception to the fast opening grab, they read fast and want depth rather than slowness. The way to hold both is a fast, legible first two seconds followed by a longer, slower build, which is roughly the shape `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` already has.

And `visuals.md` governs the frame itself. Human presence and emotional specificity are the two principles this call leans on. A face reading forty-five to fifty-five, in a specific state rather than generic positivity, with the message carried by the picture because sound is off and captions may never be seen.

*Justification:* Cost per lead says the comfortable family works and the one voice on record says it reads as a person who does not have the problem. If the second thing is true at any scale, the account is buying cheap leads from women who do not believe it, which is precisely the gate-two blind spot nobody can currently see into.

*Territory: Creators and talent.*
