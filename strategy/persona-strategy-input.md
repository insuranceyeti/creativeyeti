# Persona strategy input — Health For Moms

## The call

**Lead acquisition against `pays-and-still-owes`, and change what the account says to her. Do not change who it looks for.**

She is the mother who already has insurance and is still broke. The account already delivers to her at scale. It just talks to her as if she has no coverage, and then it sells her a feeling instead of a way out. The fix is a premise swap inside an audience the account already reaches, which is the cheapest high-leverage move on the table.

The premise the creative runs on today is that she is a woman who needs rescuing and who will enjoy telling her husband she found a deal. The premise the evidence supports is that she is a woman who has been paying every month, got nothing back, and does not know she is allowed to leave. Those are different scripts and different proof. The word "uninsured" appears in **2 of 1,342 comments**. The word "deductible" appears in **207**, both re-pinned live today. She is not a gap in the market. She is a customer of somebody else's product who is angry about it.

The message that carries her is `not-allowed-to-leave`: she can decline the plan her job offers. That message bought the cheapest lead at real scale in this account's history, **2,580 leads at $11.96 across $30,845.69**, re-pinned live today by a direct lookup on both ads. Against the trailing quarter's $22.63 that is 47.1% cheaper. Both ads are dark and have been for months.

**Make a directed, funded, sized bet on `already-knows`, the woman roughly 45 to 60. Do not lead with her.**

She is the most attentive audience this account has ever found, she absorbs about a fifth of its money, and not one ad in the library was built for her on purpose. That is real whitespace and the brand should buy some of it. But leading with her would be a mistake this quarter, and the reason is cost. The creative that actually reaches her runs **$26.58 per lead across $32,935.04 of lifetime Skit spend**, against POV's **$20.09 across $121,820.19** and the account's **$18.78 lifetime blend**, all re-pinned live today. Reaching her costs roughly a third more per lead on the only gate the brand can see, while the gate that would justify the premium is invisible. The team's stated objective is efficiency before volume. A bet is defensible on that objective. A lead call is not.

**Deprioritize the peaceful outcome montage and the `the-win-i-get-to-report` message under it.** This is the deprioritization and it is the biggest single line item in the account. Three ads carrying that idea hold **$110,205.90 of lifetime spend**, and the phrase the brand has paid to say millions of times, "wife of the year," comes back **0 times in 1,342 comments**. The lead ad, `Moms43 - 4 - V3`, holds **3.28% of viewers** in the trailing quarter against a craft floor of 12% to 15%. It is not a persona. It is a mood, and the account is buying it at full price.

Two layers, marked honestly the way the method requires. **The data layer:** the account already delivers to `pays-and-still-owes`, and her cheapest message is sitting switched off in the library. That is observed. **The decision layer:** pointing the whole top of the funnel at the switching premise, and putting real money behind a woman over 45 the account has never addressed, is a choice the brand makes, not a fact the data hands over. Say which is which in any room where this gets argued.

## Why — who the account already converts

Read delivery first, because delivery is behavior and framing is intent. That is the served-versus-buyer discipline, and it is the most reliable instrument this brand owns.

**The account's money goes to a woman aged 25 to 44 and it always has.** Lifetime, re-pinned live today: **$743,218.09 spent, 39,569 leads, $18.78 cost per lead, 96.1% female, 99.3% mobile**, with **32.5% of spend on 25-34 and 44.5% on 35-44**. That is 77.0% of every dollar in one twenty-year band. The trailing 90 days, from 2026-06-06 to 2026-09-03, tightens it: **$100,067.42, 4,421 leads, $22.63 cost per lead, 95.3% female, 81.2% aged 25 to 44**. So the served audience and the delivered audience agree, and they have agreed for the account's whole life.

**What the creative says to her is where it breaks.** The top spending ad is `Moms43 - 4 - V3`, and the media read is unambiguous rather than inferred from a name. Its visual hook is a mother and baby lying in white bedding against a soft beige background, looking peacefully into camera. Its verbal hook is the string "None (music only)". Its text hook is *"POV: Telling your husband you found better health insurance, saved $400 a month, AND the deductible is zero. Wife of the year energy. 💕"* It carries **$57,506.21 lifetime and $43,120.27 in the trailing quarter**, with a **45.36% hook rate and a 3.28% hold rate**, re-pinned live today.

Hold that against the bar. A hook rate above 30% is the floor and above 45% is strong, so this ad stops the scroll beautifully. Hold rate should sit at 12% to 15% and up. At 3.28% the account's biggest spender fails the attention bar by a factor of four while passing the stopping bar comfortably. That is a specific and readable failure: the opener earns the look and the body gives her no reason to stay. The killer-ads discipline says the first three seconds buy attention and seconds three to five have to hand over the best benefit or re-hook. A wordless montage does neither. There is no promise, no proof, and nothing to be curious about after the first frame.

**The premise is wrong in a way the scripts make checkable.** Not one of the thirteen scripts read at full media depth in `source-pulls/ad-account.md` asks whether the viewer has insurance. Every one assumes she does not, or assumes she can freely switch. Read against the awareness ladder, the account's own tags put **60.9% of lifetime spend on Problem Aware and 28.6% on Solution Aware**, with essentially nothing Unaware and 0.1% Most Aware. So the account has never once tried to reach a woman who was not already unhappy. That is fine. The problem is that the creative written for a problem-aware woman keeps offering her a product-aware reward, which is the status of having fixed it. She has not decided yet. She is still angry.

**The one time the account spoke to her real situation, it bought the cheapest lead it has ever bought.** Two statics, verified live by a direct lookup on both ad ids today. `B1 - Copy 7` carries the headline *"Left my big insurance company for a mom-focused one. 24% cheaper and I choose my own doctor 😌"*, at **$16,212.61 and 1,349 leads for $12.02 each**. `B1 samar- Copy 1` carries *"Didn't know I could ditch my job's health plan… until I did."*, at **$14,633.08 and 1,231 leads for $11.89**. Together: **$30,845.69, 2,580 leads, $11.96**.

And here is the thing this run found that the vault does not say. **Those two ads delivered 83.4% of their money to women aged 25 to 44 and only 15.7% to women 45 and over.** So `not-allowed-to-leave` is not a message that cuts evenly across two personas, which is how `personas-profile.md` files it. On the delivery evidence it is **Persona 1's message**, landing squarely in her age band, at the cheapest cost the account has ever produced at real volume. That sharpens the call rather than softening it: the flagship persona's best message is already proven, already built, and already switched off.

**One caveat the account-reading method insists on and it applies here.** Those two are statics, and statics skew toward the bottom of the funnel. A fair read groups ads by the job they do before ranking them, so a $11.96 static and a $22.63 video blend are not competing for the same thing. What keeps the comparison honest is scale and repetition: 2,580 leads is not a low-spend ad picking off the easy conversions, and the headline is a switching story rather than an offer or a discount, which is top-of-funnel work in a static wrapper. The cost gap survives the caveat. It is smaller than the raw numbers suggest, and it is still the largest one in the account.

## Why — who the customer says it is for

The persona method ranks evidence strongest first: post-purchase surveys, first-party reviews, order data, retail reviews, community comments, then category signal. **Health For Moms holds rung five of six and only rung five.** So everything in this section is a well-counted signal from one noisy source, and recurrence inside one source does not promote it. A phrase appearing 207 times is not verified. It is counted.

**She is insured, and she says so by arguing with the ad's number.** The string "mine is" appears in **82 of 1,342 comments**. An upstream pass read all 82: 76 post a specific dollar figure, and **64 of those 76, or 84%, name a number higher than the $6,000 the ad states**. She does not say her deductible is high. She says "$11,000." That precision is the tell. She has already done the arithmetic the ad is about to do for her, which is why a figure in the opener invites her to top it and quietly disqualify the offer as unrealistic.

The register is clipped, numeric and sardonic. She leads with a figure and ends with a shrug. *"Lucky! Mine is $11,000."* *"6k....try 13k!"* And the identity in nine words, from 2026-04-15: *"Why we have insurance and still have a damn bill when we go to the doctor."*

**She does not know she can leave, and a stranger has to tell her.** The word "employer" appears in **24 comments**, re-pinned live today, and that signal is the most durable thing in the corpus. It keeps appearing from March through August while nearly everything else went quiet after May. *"nope. My job picks the plan that they offer,"* 2026-03-30. *"Mine is $9,200/individual, $17,500/family and that's the only plan my employer offers 😬,"* 2026-03-27. And from 2026-08-15, still arriving in the newest window: *"Has anyone tried this? I pay quite a bit for an employee plan with a very high deductible. Is this worth it and is it widely accepted?"*

Then the moment that decides the call. On 2026-05-05 a commenter answered another commenter, verified verbatim in a live pull today: *"And if it's an employer insurance, you don't have to sign up with that employer insurance you can decline the coverage and go out and find your own insurance carrier."* The brand has never said that sentence in an ad. A customer said it, for free, under an ad the brand paid for. That is the single line the lead persona is missing, and the account already has two statics that gesture at it and $11.96 of proof that it works.

**What she does not say is as loud as what she does.** "Wife of the year" returns **0 of 1,342**, against roughly 68% of spend. "Open enrollment" returns **1**. "COBRA" returns **0**, re-pinned live today. So the brand's biggest creative bet has zero uptake in her language, and the classic switching triggers the category runs on are simply not in her mouth.

**Two groups shout and neither can buy.** The expecting mother is 42 comments across 15 ads over fourteen months. The family with a chronic condition is 55 across 17. The plans are medically underwritten and both are screened out. The method is explicit that volume and emotional intensity are ranked apart, and here they point at two different women, with the intense one ineligible. So neither becomes a persona. They are a named recruitment cost, and reaching either harder makes it worse. From 2026-04-01, 7 likes: *"your insurance says im not eligible because im pregnant 😂 huh.. so much for being for Moms 😂"*

## Why — who is missing or emerging

**The woman over 45 is the emerging bet, and this run found that the reason the account reaches her is not the one the vault records.**

The vault's finding is that format acts as an unintended age lever: Skit sends 31.5% of lifetime spend to women 45 and over, POV sends 10.6%. Both figures reproduced exactly in live pulls today. **Skit: $32,935.04, with 23.9% on 45-54, 6.8% on 55-64 and 0.8% on 65+. POV: $121,820.19, with 8.6%, 1.6% and 0.4%.** Roughly a three times gap, and it holds on denominators heavy enough to trust.

Then I looked inside the Skit tag, and the lever falls apart as a format story.

| `Moms36 - 3 - A - 2 - V4c` — "I wish this was a joke" | $13,753.29 | **40.9%** | 14.43% | $25.56 |

Two ads carry the same Skit tag and deliver **40.9%** and **14.6%** of their money to women 45 and over. The second one is *below* the account average. So "Skit reaches the older woman" is an artifact of which skits got the money: the `Moms36` pair alone is **$18,744.84 of the $32,935.04 Skit total, 56.9% of it**, at roughly 40% to the 45-and-over band, and it is dragging the whole format number up.

What separates them is visible in the media, not the label. `Moms36 - 3 - A - 2 - V4c` opens on a woman looking straight into the lens with a sigh and a frustrated expression in warm domestic light, saying *"I wish this was a joke."* `MOMS38 - 2 - V3` opens on a woman on a porch performing both halves of a phone call with her insurer, under the overlay *"When you have to call your health insurance 🙄"*. One is a woman confessing her own situation to camera. The other is a comedy sketch about a situation. **The confession finds the woman over 45. The sketch does not.** That is a sharper and more usable read than the format lever, and it is new to this run.

It also breaks a comfortable story. Nearly every one of the roughly 12 clear recognitions in 1,342 comments sits on the denial sketch, including *"'It's out of network' 'But it's in the same building!' 'In a different hallway' 🤣🤣🤣🤣🤣 whoever came up with this ad deserves a raise!! Love this!"* from 2026-04-03. `personas-profile.md` reads those recognitions as evidence for the older woman. The delivery says the sketch is landing on a 35 to 44 audience, at 50.4% of its spend. So the recognition and the age split sit on two different ads and probably two different women. Nobody should build a 45-plus brief on the denial sketch's comment section.

**What she asks for, in her own words, and nobody answers.** Sixteen comments across 7 ads ask politely whether she still counts. From a live pull today, the string "still qualify" returns exactly **1 of 1,342**: *"What if your kids are over 18, do I still qualify as a Mom?"*, 2026-04-29. And the fullest version, 2026-04-26, 2 likes: *"OK, this is great for mom's of young children. What about the rest of us? ... What about those of us whose children are grown and moved out of the house now? My deductible used to be reasonable, now it's almost $7000 a year."* The tone is the tell. She is not complaining. She is asking, and no one replies on the thread.

The word "mom" is doing two jobs. The brand means an identity. She hears an eligibility rule, and she concludes she has aged out. Her economics say the opposite: a woman in her late forties with a $7,000 deductible is healthy enough to pass underwriting and expensive enough to care, which puts her closer to the product's real buyer than a 28-year-old with a newborn.

**The account tried her twice, for $144.07, and both files are paused.** `MOMS 28 - FOLDER 2 - AD 1` opens on a woman in her fifties in a cozy home saying *"At 55, I know better than to waste money on coverage that doesn't cover."* It spent **$109.86** and put **53.4% of that on women 45 and over**, the highest share of any ad this run examined, on a 32.98% hook rate and an 11.31% hold rate. Its sibling `AD 2` spent **$34.21** at 32.5%. Together, 4 leads at $36.02. That is far too little to prove a cost, and it is the clearest signal in the account that an explicit age callout moves delivery, which is exactly what the older-audience method predicts. Direct callouts pull relevance for this group and subtlety underperforms. She needs to be told plainly the ad is for her.

**Why she is a bet worth funding rather than a curiosity.** She tolerates more narrative, not less, so the long problem setup this account has never built is a fit rather than a waste. She converts from further up the funnel, which suits an account already sitting 60.9% Problem Aware. She trusts an authority or something that looks like a report over a trend, and the brand owns a compliant one it has never used: a real licensed partner agent, already inside the funnel. And she is value-conscious, which pairs badly with the savings claim the brand cannot substantiate and well with the Approved State List, the one owned device that qualifies a viewer in the first frame.

**What is genuinely missing, and stays missing.** None of the ten highest-spending ads opens on a life event. "COBRA" is 0, "laid off" is 0, "divorce" is 0, "turning 26" is 0, all in 1,342. In $743,218.09 of lifetime creative the account has never opened on the moment a woman's coverage changed. That is an absence where presence would be expected in this category, and it is not a persona. It is a doorway nobody has walked through, and it belongs to whichever persona the brand leads with.

## The size of it

Size before you recommend. A buyer who is real but small is not the lead.

All re-pinned live today from the account summary. Read the two columns together, because the movement is the point. **The older woman's share of delivery fell from 21.2% lifetime to 16.2% in the trailing quarter.** Nobody decided that. The format mix moved to POV, POV sends 10.6% of its money to her, and the delivery followed the creative. Read through the breakdown effect, Meta pushed budget into the pocket it predicted would stay cheapest, and that pocket happened to be the one with the most files in it. She is not shrinking as a buyer. She is shrinking as a thing the account buys.

**On volume, this is not close.** `pays-and-still-owes` is roughly four to five times the older woman by delivered spend, and she is the biggest identity in the comment corpus by a wide margin: 207 deductible mentions and 82 unprompted dollar disclosures against 16 eligibility questions. On the corpus alone the gap would look like thirteen to one.

**On emotional intensity the ranking inverts, and both facts matter.** The older woman watches three to five times longer than anyone the account courts. Hold rates on the creative that reaches her run 13.06% to 14.43% on the `Moms36` pair against 3.28% on the top spender, all re-pinned live. She clears the craft floor. The flagship's creative does not. Volume sizes the opportunity; intensity points at advocacy, message testing and creator-led work. Flattening those two rankings into one would hide the whole diagnosis, so they are kept apart here.

**The comment corpus systematically undercounts her, and the direction of the error is knowable.** She is roughly 16 comments against a body of viewers absorbing a fifth of the spend and watching several times longer. The older-audience method describes a group that follows a long story rather than performing in a thread, so an instrument that counts typing will miss a viewer who watches. Sizing her from comment volume would badly undercount her. Sizing her from delivery is the better instrument, and delivery still puts her at about a fifth.

**And the cost of her is the number that decides the call.**

That is the price of the bet, stated plainly. Reaching the older woman costs roughly a third more per lead than POV and about 42% more than the account's lifetime blend, on gate one, with gate two invisible. The team's own sequence is efficiency first, then scale. A persona that costs more per lead cannot be handed the lead slot under that sequence unless someone can show the leads are better, and nobody can see whether they are.

## Alternatives considered and why not

**Lead with `already-knows`, the woman 45 and over.** This is the runner-up and it is a genuine one. Her case is the strongest single instrument in the whole persona system, because the age split comes from Meta delivery rather than from the comment corpus, and it reproduces two independent ways. She holds attention where the flagship's creative does not. She has never had an ad built for her. Three things hold her out of the lead slot. She is about a fifth of the delivery and falling. She costs 41.5% more per lead than the account blend on the only gate anyone can see. And her supporting corpus is 16 comments, which is a candidate rather than a pattern. Delivery tells you who was served; it cannot tell you who bought. She gets the bet, not the lead.

**Promote the mom on her job's plan to a persona and lead with her.** Tempting, and the money is real: $11.96 across 2,580 leads is the cheapest thing this account has ever done at scale. The personas pass demoted her to a behavioral signal for failing durability, and that call is right. Being locked into an employer plan ends when she changes jobs, so it describes a situation rather than a person, and the method is explicit that a buying behavior dressed as a persona is the most common way this work goes wrong. This run adds a second reason to keep her demoted: her ads delivered 83.4% to women 25 to 44, which is the flagship's own band. She is not a separate buyer. She is the flagship's best message. Leading with her as a persona would invent a customer and hide that the real finding is a premise fix.

**Lead with `built-it-herself`, the self-employed mother.** Held at thin confidence and it stays there. The string "self employed" returns 2 comments in 1,342 and one of those is a rival agent's pitch, so there is exactly one genuine self-identification. She has 0.25% of lifetime spend behind her. Her one static returned $16.77 on $1,878.04, inside the good band on volume too thin to call. And her whole story is a comparison between two things the brand's own compliance rules forbid naming, so the sharpest version of her message is against the rules before it is written. She is the one persona where a stated ICP and the comment evidence agree, which is worth something. Six rows is still a candidate.

**Lead with the expecting mother.** On buyer evidence she would be first: the widest and longest-running objection in the corpus, 42 comments across 15 ads over fourteen months, and 63.2% of the category's organic feed. On spend she is a perfect zero, lifetime, on every field. In a normal account that is textbook whitespace. It is not whitespace here, because the plans are medically underwritten and the funnel turns her away in public using the brand's own name as the punchline. A persona requires a buyer. She is a recruitment cost, and the same reasoning retires the family with a chronic condition.

**Keep leading with the peaceful outcome and treat the montage as the safe default.** This is the status quo and it is the alternative with the most money riding on it, so it deserves a straight answer. It fails on every instrument available at once. The message returns zero uptake in 1,342 comments. The lead ad holds 3.28% against a 12% floor. And the sharpest thing anyone has said about the footage, on 2026-09-03, is *"I'm sure the mom that can afford matching outfits prob just pays in cash 🤦‍♀️."* There is no retention story to rescue it either, because the account has zero purchase events, no repeat buyer and 0.1% of lifetime spend at the most-aware stage. Every dollar here is acquisition, so this mismatch is an acquisition mismatch with nothing behind it.

## Confidence and what would raise it

**Confidence in the lead call: mixed, and it cannot be higher on the evidence that exists.** That is the ceiling on every persona in this brain and this recommendation does not get an exemption. What the call actually rests on is two things of different quality, and they should be read differently.

The **delivery half is solid**. Who the account reaches, what each format costs, which ad delivered which age band, what the switching statics returned: all of it is Meta-reported, re-pinned live today, and sits on denominators in the tens of thousands of dollars. The account genuinely does put 77.0% of its money on women 25 to 44 and genuinely did buy 2,580 leads at $11.96.

The **identity half is one noisy source**. That she is insured rather than uninsured, that she does not know she can leave, that she guards her phone number, that the older woman thinks she has aged out: every one of those is a count from comments under the brand's own paid ads, with a null author on every row, 60.1% of them on a single creative family and 64.0% inside two months. The counts are honest. The inference from a count to a person is not checkable, and nothing in this build can make it so.

**What would raise it, ranked by how much the answer would move this doc.**

**One, and it is worth more than everything else combined: gate two.** Ask the partner agencies who actually calls back and enrolls, and what she said on the call. That single answer supplies the top rung of the evidence ladder this brand has never had, a real buyer tied to a real reason, and it would replace both missing tiers at once. It is routed to the brand because no tool reaches it.

**Two: whether the older woman closes.** The whole case against leading with her is that she costs 41.5% more per lead. If the agencies report that leads from the grievance and confession creative book calls and enroll at a materially better rate than leads from the montage, that premium is not a cost, it is a discount, and **the lead call in this document inverts.** That is the single cleanest way this recommendation is wrong, and it is stated here so it can be checked rather than defended.

**Three: an independent read of how mothers in this category talk.** Reviews are empty, Reddit is unreachable, and the one tracked competitor sells life insurance. Any brand-free source of category language would let the persona system move off a surface the brand pays for, targets into and can moderate. Until one exists, mixed is the honest ceiling no matter how large the comment corpus grows.

**Four, and it is cheap: one open-text box on the thank-you page.** Ask a woman in her own words why she just filled the form in. That would create this brand's first record of a confirmed speaker, and it would begin closing the stated-versus-revealed blank that currently makes every motivation claim in this brain a hypothesis about a person nobody has met.

**What would not raise it:** more comments. The corpus grew by 20 rows in a single day between builds and the ceiling did not move, because recurrence inside one source is still one source.

## Open loops

**1. Two ads with the same format tag reach women twenty years apart in age.**

`Moms36 - 3 - A - 2 - V4c` opens on a woman looking straight into the lens with a sigh, saying "I wish this was a joke," and puts 40.9% of its $13,753.29 on women 45 and over. `MOMS38 - 2 - V4` opens on a woman on a porch performing both sides of a call with her insurer, and puts 12.4% of its $2,172.45 there, which is below the account's own 21.2%. Both carry the Skit tag. Both were made in the same account within two months of each other.

*Pull: Surprise.* It fired when I opened the Skit format number that two vault documents rest on and found the tag was hiding two opposite behaviors, with one pair of files carrying 56.9% of the format's spend and nearly all of its age lift.

*Question:* What is it about a woman talking straight to camera about her own bill that finds a viewer over 45?

*Why it matters:* the brand is about to spend money on the older woman, and the current instruction in the vault is to make skits. If the lever is the confession rather than the sketch, then a whole production lane gets briefed differently and the cheapest version of the bet is a single woman and a phone rather than a two-part comedy setup.

*Territory: Personas.*

**2. The cheapest lead this account ever bought went to the youngest band it reaches.**

The two switching statics returned 2,580 leads at $11.96 across $30,845.69, and sent 83.4% of that money to women aged 25 to 44 against just 15.7% to women 45 and over. Every other document in this brain files the employer-plan signal as something that cuts across both the flagship and the older woman.

*Pull: Tension.* The vault says this message belongs to two personas and the delivery says it belongs almost entirely to one, and both cannot be a fair description of who that headline finds.

*Question:* Who is the woman that the "you can leave your job's plan" message actually finds?

*Why it matters:* it decides whether restarting those statics is a flagship play or a way to reach the older woman cheaply. If it is the flagship's message, the older woman still needs her own creative built from scratch and the bet costs what the Skit numbers say it costs.

*Territory: Personas.*

**3. The account has said a woman's age out loud exactly twice, for $144.07.**

`MOMS 28 - FOLDER 2 - AD 1` opens with "At 55, I know better than to waste money on coverage that doesn't cover" and put 53.4% of its $109.86 on women 45 and over, the highest share of any ad examined in this run. Its sibling put 32.5% of $34.21 there. Both were paused. Across 147 delivering ads, every other face on camera reads as a woman in her thirties.

*Pull: Gap.* There is real evidence sitting here that a spoken age changes who Meta finds, and in twenty-one months nothing has ever been done with it.

*Question:* What happens to who this account reaches when the woman on camera is the same age as the woman watching?

*Why it matters:* the bet on the older woman needs a face, and right now the brand has no evidence about casting beyond two files that ran for the price of a dinner. If age-matched casting is the lever, the production ask is a different creator rather than a different script.

*Territory: Creators and talent.*

**4. The ad that earns agreement and the ad that reaches the older woman are not the same ad.**

Nearly every one of the roughly 12 clear recognitions in 1,342 comments sits on the denial sketch, `MOMS38 - 2`, which holds 24.48% of viewers and delivers 50.4% of its spend to women 35 to 44. The confession skit reaches the older woman at 40.9% and earns almost no recognition comments at all.

*Pull: Pattern.* The same mismatch shows up on every ad in the sketch family once the delivery split is read next to the comment thread, which turns one odd ad into a property of the whole lane.

*Question:* Who is recognizing herself in the denial sketch?

*Why it matters:* the brand's best-held creative is being used as evidence for a persona it may not be reaching. If the woman laughing at that sketch is the flagship rather than the older woman, then the account's strongest attention asset belongs to the persona this doc recommends leading with, and it should be funded on that basis.

*Territory: Personas.*
