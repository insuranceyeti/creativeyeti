# Organic channels inventory - Health For Moms

## Headline read

**Health For Moms has no organic social presence. That is the finding, and it is not a data gap.**

The brand owns exactly one social surface Parker can find: a Facebook page at `facebook.com/p/Health-For-Moms-61569029311372/`, listed in Orlando FL, which exists so the ads have somewhere to run from. **Verified** in `reputation-analysis.md`, where a dedicated search for the brand's Instagram and TikTok presence returned nothing about the company and the Facebook page was the only brand-owned surface that surfaced anywhere. There is no TikTok handle, no discoverable Instagram account, no YouTube channel, no Pinterest, no brand-run group and no newer community app. There is no posting cadence to read, no follower count to weigh engagement against, and no organic post anywhere for a strategist to describe.

**One thing has to be said plainly at the top, because it is the mistake most likely to be made from this brain.** Parker holds a TikTok library of 23 videos for this brand. **None of them belong to Health For Moms.** Every one is a video by a stranger about health insurance, medical bills or the cost of having a baby, scraped from an eighteen-phrase keyword set on 2026-09-03. I re-checked it live on 2026-09-04 and the top five by view count belong to `@drashleehendry`, `@paumod`, `@kaseyjaneanderson`, `@salina_sunshine` and `@readra21`, all unaffiliated creators, all with `is_ad` recorded as false. **Verified.** That library is a category mining feed for paid creative inspiration. Any sentence that reads it as this brand's organic performance would be a fabrication.

**So the inventory below is nearly empty, and the empty version is the correct one.** What can honestly be said is this: the brand is a paid-only operation. It reaches roughly 4.36 million impressions a quarter and builds essentially no owned audience out of them. Its only two-way surface with a mother is the comment thread under an ad it paid for. And the platform where its category's attention actually concentrates, TikTok, is one it has never posted on.

## Platform inventory

### Facebook — the ad page

**Active or dormant.** Alive only in the sense that ads run from it. No organic posting cadence is visible on any surface Parker can reach, and no organic post was found by search. **Data-limited** on whether organic posts exist behind a wall Parker cannot open, and **verified** that a dedicated search surfaced none.

**Following and engagement pattern.** Follower count **not available**. Facebook is blocked to web fetch from this session and no organic-social tool is connected. What is available is an indirect and quite exact read from the ad account, and it is the single most useful number in this doc. Over the 90 days to 2026-09-03, the account's top-spending ad bought **1,987,158 impressions and produced 116 page likes**. That is a page-like rate of **0.0058%**, or roughly one new follower for every 17,000 impressions. Its two nearest siblings produced 13 page likes on 327,819 impressions and 10 page likes on 304,713. **Verified**, engagement pull 2026-09-04. Read against `organic-social-analysis.md`, which says engagement only means something once it is read against the account's size and cadence, the honest version here is starker: the account has no cadence, so the only follower growth it gets is the residue of paid delivery, and that residue is a rounding error.

**Roughly what it is doing.** It is a delivery identity, not an account. Its job is to be the name and avatar attached to a paid post so the ad has a sender. **Inferred**, confidence **strong**, from the absence of any discoverable organic post plus the presence of a fully-funded ad operation running from it.

**Real people or product-only.** Not readable as an organic question, because there are no organic posts. What appears under this page's name in the feed is paid creative, and that is the paid doc's territory rather than this one's.

**Broadcast versus community.** This is where the page has an actual, measurable signal, and it is worth recording carefully because it is the only community evidence the brand owns. The page's ads have drawn **1,322 comments lifetime and 92 in the trailing 90 days**. **Verified** from the ad-comment corpus. The shape of them is the finding. **Fifty of the 92 are a bare "Help" or "Info" with nothing else attached**, and in August specifically, 36 of 59 comments, or 61.0%, were the single word "Help", every one of them sitting on the same ad. Nothing in that ad's copy or any hook field asks anyone to comment "Help." **Verified** by a full comment pull.

So there is a real two-way surface here, and the brand is not working it. Three dozen mothers a month are raising a hand under a paid post, in a channel with no visible route to an agent and no reply routine. You gotta be perfectly healthy to qualify it looks like."*, *"Preexisting health condition of cancer disqualified me"*, *"Is this insurance or something like a Christian health share?"* and, from a self-identified licensed health insurance advisor, *"never put your info online unless you want 100 calls a day about health insurance."* **Verified** verbatims. Read through the customer-as-hero lens in `organic-social-analysis.md`, this is the purest broadcast posture available: the brand talks, mothers answer, and nobody answers them back.

**Who it targets.** Not readable from organic content, because there is none. The audience the *page* reaches is set entirely by paid delivery.

**Strength read: 2 out of 10.** The two points are for existing and for having a live, genuinely active comment surface with real customer language in it. Everything above two would require posting, a following, a reply routine or a reason for a mother to visit the page rather than land on it. None of those is present. Confidence **mixed**, because the page itself could not be opened and a modest organic history behind that wall would move the score to a three or four without changing the read.

**One line: an address, not a home.**

## Organic home and cross-platform footprint

**There is no organic home. There is not even a candidate.**

The usual shape of this section is a brand with a clear center and a couple of neglected satellites. This brand has one surface, it does not post on it, and it exists so ads have a sender. Nothing spreads attention thin here, because there is no attention to spread.

What sits in place of a footprint is a single funnel: a paid Meta impression, a click, a `/save/` landing page, a form, then a phone call at a partner insurance agency. Every step of that is bought. **Verified** across the account pulls and the sibling performance docs. A mother who wants to check this company out before she hands over her details has nowhere brand-owned to go except a Facebook page that carries ads and a website. `reputation-analysis.md` measured what she finds if she searches instead: the FBI's page on emerging discount medical insurance scams, the FTC's page on spotting health insurance scams, and a Tennessee Attorney General page, before the brand's own site. **Verified** from the live result set.

That is the cross-platform read in one sentence: **the brand has built a very efficient way to be found by a mother who was not looking for it, and no way at all to be checked out by a mother who then wants to.**

## Community and the broadcast-versus-include read

**Pure broadcast, with one genuinely alive comment thread the brand does not answer.**

The evidence is the 1,322-comment lifetime corpus under the ads, and its shape is unusual enough to describe properly. It is heavily lopsided: 795 of the 1,322, or 60.1%, sit on one creative family, and 846 of 1,322, or 64.0%, fall in March and April 2026. **Verified** by two independent counts in the whitespace analysis. In the trailing 90 days, 66 of 92 comments sat on the single top-spending ad.

Inside that thread three things happen at once, and only one of them is the brand's doing.

Mothers ask for help. Fifty of 92 in the window are a bare "Help" or "Info." **Verified.** Nothing in the creative prompts it, which makes it the clearest unprompted signal in the corpus: these are women trying to start a conversation with a company that has not opened one.

Mothers argue with the offer. *"I called them they spammed me and wasted hours of my time to tell me it was going to cost $400/month LOL. SO IT DOESNT SAVE YOU $400 it literally costs just the same if not more than regular scammy health insurance."*, posted 2026-09-03, sits directly under the savings claim that carries most of the account's spend. **Verified** verbatim.

And mothers teach each other. The comment threads carry corrections, eligibility rules and warnings passed between strangers, including a licensed advisor telling viewers not to submit their details. That is community forming under a brand's post without the brand in it.

Against the customer-as-hero read in `organic-social-analysis.md`, this is the weakest posture available: the brand is the hero of every post, the customer appears only in the replies, and the replies go unanswered. Confidence **strong**, because the corpus is large, live, and the only voice-of-customer surface this brand has, since reviews and post-purchase surveys are both verified empty.

## Who the organic content is targeting

**There is no organic content, so there is no organic targeting signal. That is the honest answer and it should not be dressed up.**

All three layers the method asks for come back empty for the brand's own channels. There is no visual world, because there are no posts. There are no creators or faces, because nobody appears on a brand-owned organic surface. There is no verbal or text layer, because there are no captions.

What can be said, carefully and with its boundary marked, is what the one owned surface *transmits* without meaning to. The Facebook page's public face in the feed is whatever ad is running, and over the trailing 90 days that delivered to an audience that is **95.3% female, 81.2% aged 25 to 44 and 99.5% mobile**, split 60.3% Facebook to 39.1% Instagram. **Verified**, live pull 2026-09-04. But that is paid delivery, decided by the auction and the creative, and it belongs to the paid docs. It is not an organic audience signal and it must not be read as one, because an organic signal is something the brand chose to publish and this is something the brand bought.

**The gap this creates is the point of the section.** In an ideal footprint, the organic targeting signal and the paid targeting signal can be checked against each other, and a divergence is a finding. Here there is nothing to check. The paid engine is running with no organic signal alongside it, so nobody can tell whether the brand's own published voice would reach the same woman its ads reach.

## Organic-to-paid signal

**Starving, not feeding, and the amount it is starving by can be measured.**

The mechanism matters more than the verdict. A platform running a new ad can use a brand's own organic presence as one input for who to show it to: the engagement it gets, the people visibly shown, the language used. Strong organic hands the delivery system a richer starting picture. Thin organic does not make paid impossible, it removes a helpful source of signal and makes the creative work harder on its own.

This brand hands the system nothing. There is no organic post, no owned engagement, no visible community and no published language for the platform to read. Every signal Meta has about who Health For Moms is for comes from the ads themselves and from who converted on them.

The measurable version of that is the page-like number. **1,987,158 impressions on the top ad produced 116 page likes, alongside 327 shares, 385 saves and 84 comments.** **Verified.** Two million impressions is a genuinely large amount of attention, and the owned audience it built is about a hundred people. So the account is not only starting without an organic signal, it is not accumulating one either. Twenty-one months and $743,218 of spend later, the brand's owned surface is still a page nobody visits on purpose.

Two consequences worth carrying forward, both marked as reads rather than instructions, since this doc presents rather than prescribes.

**The paid engine is doing all the qualifying alone.** With no organic seed, the creative's first two seconds are the only thing telling the platform who this is for. That is consistent with what the account actually shows: delivery is 95.3% female because a hook that opens *"POV: Telling your husband..."* names its audience in four words, not because an owned audience taught the system anything.

**The one asset the brand is accumulating is a comment corpus, and it is accumulating it in a channel it does not work.** 1,322 comments is a real body of customer language, and it is currently the brand's only voice-of-customer surface, since reviews and post-purchase surveys are both verified empty. It is being generated as a byproduct of paid delivery and left where it lands.

**Where the deep read belongs.** There is no brand organic to hand to the organic-social team for a teardown, so the pointer runs the other way. The live surface worth a deeper read is the ad comment corpus, which belongs to the voice-of-customer and community work rather than to an organic audit. And the category feed in Parker's TikTok library already has its own deep read at `audits/2026-09/monthly-organic-tiktok-audit.md`, which is correctly framed there as a category and inspiration read rather than as brand organic.

## Open loops

**1. The category's whole conversation happens on a platform this brand has never posted on.**
Parker's mining library holds 23 videos in this category and 19 score as relevant. Twelve of the 19 are anchored to pregnancy, delivery costs or a baby's first year, and one video of a doctor cold-calling hospitals for cash prices holds 6.2 million views, which is 66.1% of every view in the library. Health For Moms has no TikTok presence at all, and its paid library returns zero results for the keyword "pregnant" across every field, lifetime.
*Pull: Gap.* It fired when the library's own composition showed where this category's attention actually concentrates, and the brand's footprint had nothing standing in that place.
**Question: Where do mothers in this category go to figure out what health insurance actually costs?**
The brand currently reaches a mother only in a paid feed and has nowhere to be found when she goes looking on her own, so knowing where she looks decides whether the missing footprint is a real cost or an irrelevance.
*Territory: Personas.*

**2. Two million impressions bought about a hundred followers.**
The account's top-spending ad delivered 1,987,158 impressions over 90 days and produced 116 page likes, a rate of 0.0058%, alongside 327 shares and 385 saves. Its two nearest siblings produced 13 and 10 page likes on roughly 300,000 impressions each.
*Pull: Surprise.* An account spending at this level would normally leave some owned audience behind as a byproduct, and after twenty-one months and $743,218 this one has left almost none.
**Question: Why does almost nobody who sees these ads follow the brand?**
The platform reads a brand's owned audience as one of the signals it uses to seed delivery, so an account that reaches millions and accumulates nobody is starting every new ad from a colder position than its spend suggests.
*Territory: Messaging.*

**3. The category's most-watched voices speak for themselves, and this brand's never do.**
Every creator in the mining library is a woman talking about her own bill, her own delivery, her own uninsured family, in her own room with no music. The single biggest video in the category is a doctor doing her own investigation on camera. Meanwhile every face in this brand's 147 delivering ads is a hired mother reading a script, and the brand has no organic surface where an unpaid voice could appear at all.
*Pull: Pattern.* The same shape recurs across nineteen unaffiliated videos from nineteen different creators, and the brand's own footprint has no version of it anywhere.
**Question: Who are the women that mothers in this category actually listen to about money and health?**
The brand names casting as its top bottleneck, and the answer decides whether the person it needs is someone it hires or someone it has no relationship with yet.
*Territory: Creators and talent.*
