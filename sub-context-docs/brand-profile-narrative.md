## The four things that matter most

**Cost per lead, the one number this team steers by, is nearly blind to the creative.** It moved from $22.65 to $22.63 across two structurally different quarters. In between, spend fell 73.5%, ad count fell 70.4%, the dominant format collapsed into one container at roughly 70% of spend, the dominant emotion swapped from anger at 55.2% to pride at 49.5%, and spend-weighted hold rate fell from 11.09% to 3.83%. A number that survives that much upheaval is not measuring the creative. It is measuring the offer, and the offer did not change. **The account switched off its cheaper half.** Lifetime, statics carry **529 ads, $168,907.73, 13,735 leads at a $12.30 cost per lead**, against video's $574,310.36 and 25,834 leads at $22.23. Statics took 22.7% of the money and delivered 34.7% of the leads. Not one has run in the last 90 days. Three separate 90-day audits describe this account as having never run a static; a lifetime pull overturned that. This is a production-proven modality that went dark around March, not an untested idea. ## Who the brand is and what it actually sells

Health For Moms is a health insurance matching service for mothers, run by Insurance Yeti, LLC out of Orlando. It sells nothing. A mom answers about four questions on `healthformoms.co/save/`, and her details go to a partner insurance agency where a licensed agent tries to enroll her in a private, medically underwritten plan. The brand is paid for the handoff. Its self-positioning is unusually tidy for a brand this young: one tagline, "Better family health coverage for less," one named mechanism, the MomSmart Coverage Method™, a voice rule that it "speaks mom-to-mom, not insurance-to-customer," and a compliance section it heads "THIS SECTION IS NON-NEGOTIABLE." The fuzziness is not in what the brand thinks it is. It is in what backs the claims. Every credibility marker is self-asserted, "up to 30%" has no stated basis of comparison anywhere, and nine of its ten highest-spending statics carried a flat savings claim against the brand's own hard rule. [`stated` for the positioning, `verified` for the copy audit — depth in `brand-identity-analysis.md` and `website-and-product-audit.md`]

## The customer

Four sources describe her and they disagree, which is the finding. **The brand says** she is 28 to 50, married or partnered, one to three kids, $50K to $150K+, with a hand-typed Slack override that single moms are fine "as long as we also use lingo about them not being broke." One of its five canonical ICPs turns out to be Parker's own Slack text the brand adopted, confirmed by exact substring match, so the stated set cannot serve as an independent check. **The ad account delivers** 95.3% female, 81.2% aged 25 to 44, 99.5% mobile, 60% Facebook. **Commenters under the brand's paid ads** describe someone else: "uninsured" appears in 2 of 1,342 comments and "deductible" in 207, and 82 rows answer the ad's $6,000 figure with their own, 84% of them higher. Her register is clipped, numeric and sardonic. She leads with a figure and ends with a shrug. ## Strengths to build on, exposures to manage

The real strengths are concrete and mostly sitting idle. The **Approved State List** is a genuine owned device: the one ad that puts it in the first frame, `moms-53 3`, holds the account's best CTR at 3.15% and cheapest link click at $0.69, and it qualifies the viewer before she leaves at the three-to-four second mark where every other qualifier lands too late. The account **stops the scroll beautifully**, with a spend-weighted hook rate of 43.40% against a 30% craft floor. **Changing the picture works**: the identical POV husband line bought a $23.37 lead over April's bedroom footage and a $13.24 lead over August's outdoor footage in the same month, the only proven step change in cost this account has produced in six months. And the static library is inventory, not a bet. The exposures mirror them. **Hold rate is 3.83% against a 12% to 15% floor**, so the account holds almost nobody. **Concentration is severe**: four ads sharing one text hook carried 60.5% of the quarter, and under Meta's entity grouping that is likely fewer distinct things than the count suggests, which caps incremental reach and pushes frequency onto the same pool of mothers. **Nobody credentialed has ever been on camera** across 147 delivering ads, in a category where the buyer is actively searching whether the company is real. And there is **one channel**: paid Meta carries 100% of measured spend, with two ads already DISAPPROVED and no second surface to fall back to. ## Operating constraints

No agency has ever been stated, though at least four outside creative signatures appear in the account and roughly 49 new ads arrive a month, so most production is bought. Every campaign carries a `USHA` prefix, which reads as USHEALTH Advisors and is inferred from naming only. The attribution stack is Meta Ads Manager and nothing else: Northbeam is not connected, four pixel events are auto-detected with zero formulas, and `Call` fired 42 times against 4,336 leads. Spend runs about $42,700 a month after a 73.5% quarter-over-quarter cut nobody has explained, against an April peak of $156,716. The claims envelope is the brand's own and it is strict: no government or ACA references, no flat savings claim, no named competitor, no fake doctor. The blocking unknown is unit economics — no customer value, no margin, no max tolerable cost per lead — so "efficient" here means better than the account's own trailing number, never profitable. One live fault: eleven active ads point at `go.healthformoms.co/save/`, which converts 9.1% of its link clicks against 84.6% for everything else, while `quiz.healthformoms.com` is a brand-new test at $138.27 lifetime and is unmeasured, not leaking. ## The open-loops roll-up

Three questions surface only when the sub-docs are read side by side. The single-doc versions stay in their own docs.

**1. The account's cheapest leads and its least qualifiable leads may be the same leads.** Cost per lead barely moved through a total creative overhaul, which says it is measuring the offer rather than the creative. The statics that bought the cheapest leads at $12.30 named a specific person in a specific moment. And the largest cluster of substantive comments in the 90-day window, ten of 38, is people finding out after clicking that they do not qualify on grounds no ad mentions. Nobody has put those three facts next to each other.
*Pull: Tension.* It fired because two things this brain treats as settled cannot both be right: the account ranks creative confidently on cost per lead while the funnel appears to turn away a real share of what that money buys.
*Question: What is this account actually buying when it buys a cheap lead?* Every ranking, scale call and brief in this brain is graded on gate one alone. If the cheapest ads recruit the people the funnel rejects, the ranking inverts and roughly two thirds of the budget sits behind the wrong creative. **Routed to the brand**, because only the partner agencies hold it. *Territory: Product.*

**2. Five docs each found one thing this account proved and then switched off, and none could see the pattern.** Statics at $12.30 a lead, dark since roughly March. The anger lane that carried 55.2% of spend and held 17.12% of viewers, at 2.6% this quarter with no recorded decision. The claymation set retired on $516.86 while holding 13.59%. The employer-plan static at $11.96 across 2,580 leads, dark for months. Fifteen skit files launched 2026-08-31 that drew $4.23 in five days. A pediatrician filmed in a lab coat who never speaks.
*Pull: Pattern.* It fired because the same shape keeps appearing across independent audits, formats and years, which is what a real behavior looks like rather than six unrelated calls.
*Question: What decides that a proven thing in this account stops running?* The answer separates a media-buying mechanic from a human decision. If the breakdown effect is pacing budget away from anything new before it can be measured, every format test before November dies unmeasured and the fix is structural. If people are switching things off on purpose, the reasons are hard constraints nobody has written down. *Territory: Product.*

**3. Being a company nobody can look up has a price, and nothing in this brain measures it.** Search puts FBI and FTC scam pages ahead of the brand's own site. Zero brand replies in 1,342 comments while rival brokers answer in the brand's place. Regulators are actively teaching this audience to spot exactly this kind of business. Viewers call the creative AI-made and tie it to distrust unprompted. Nobody credentialed has appeared in 147 delivering ads, while the one compliant authority the brand already owns, a real licensed partner advisor, has never been on camera. Every doc names the cost as real and none can size it.
*Pull: Gap.* It fired because there is a great deal of evidence here and nothing has ever been done with it, in a category where the buyer's own documented first move is to check whether the company exists.
*Question: How much of this account's cost per lead is the price of being a company nobody can look up?* Hook rates are healthy, so attention is not the binding constraint. If trust is, the next production budget buys a different kind of proof rather than a better hook. *Territory: Messaging.*
