# Visual vocabulary: Health For Moms

## Headline read

Health For Moms films one thing over and over: a woman alone in a bright room in her own house, holding a phone at arm's length, with a block of text sitting on top of her. Everything the brand actually argues with is laid over that frame as a graphic, not staged in it. A screenshot of a stranger's angry post, a wall of state names with green checkmarks, captions that arrive one phrase at a time. What the brand owns that nobody else in its category owns is the Approved State List: a document the viewer scans for her own state, which it has now shot four different ways and which produced the cheapest attention in the account.

The interesting part is what the last three months hide. Read the 90-day window alone and this looks like a narrow account that only knows one shot. Read the full library and it is much wider than that. This brand has already filmed a street interview in a public park with a branded microphone, a claymation world with a personified medical bill, a doctor in an exam room, a nail salon, a two-role phone-call skit that holds 27.31% of viewers, and 529 statics that brought leads at $12.30 each. The clearest expansion frontier is not a shot the brand has never seen. It is the shots it has already proven and stopped making.

### How this was read

I started with the own account and went widest there, which is what the method demands, because everything in it is in play by definition. The video window is 2026-06-06 to 2026-09-03, and inside it the account ran **147 delivering video ads carrying $100,065.31, 4,358,573 impressions and 4,421 leads at a $22.63 cost per lead**, with a **1.80% CTR** and a **$22.96 CPM**. *Verified* from `search_facebook_ads_sql`. I read the top 15 by period spend, because spend is Meta's own vote on the most potent creative, then the top 12 by period hook rate over a $100 floor, because that is where the strongest scroll-stops hide at low budget. The two lists overlap on six ads, and the six they do not share are the most useful rows in this doc.

Then I stopped trusting the window. Three separate things in the upstream audits looked like absences, and two of them turned out to be dormancy rather than absence. The 90-day diversity audit records that this account has never run a static. That is true of the window and false of the account: a lifetime static pull returns **529 ads, $168,907.73 and 13,735 leads at a $12.30 cost per lead**, and a 365-day pull returns **$74,539.28 and 5,712 leads at $13.05**, while a 180-day pull returns **zero**. *Verified* on three matched pulls. So the brand's single-frame grammar is production-proven and has simply been dark since roughly early March. The same thing happened with Street Interview, which the diversity audit listed as a dropped format. A tag-filtered pull returns **8 ads and $1,172.43 lifetime**, and when I opened the media, one of them is a young man in a public park holding a microphone branded "Weekly Health," interviewing two women in their forties or fifties who are standing in the same frame as him. *Verified* from the visual hook fields and from a frame-level read of `Moms40 - 3 - V4`. That single check moved public locations, a male on camera and two people in one shot out of the frontier and into the proven baseline, which is why the method insists the criterion is read from the own account rather than asserted.

I did not read ad names. Every visual below rests on a media field that describes what a viewer sees: `visual_hook`, `text_hook`, `verbal_hook`, `creator_demographic`, `ad_summary`, `ad_analysis`, or a full frame-level pass. I ran `analyze_video_from_url` directly on three units, chosen because they carry the most weight: the account's biggest spender, its best-holding creative, and the street interview whose classification I was about to change. Those three passes are where the caption timings, the colour of the state list, the absence of music and the shape of the public shoot come from.

Last, the orbit. Parker's TikTok mining library holds 23 videos, 19 scored Relevant, and I read the top three by view count at full report depth in this run against the full 23-video read already in `audits/2026-09/monthly-organic-tiktok-audit.md`. One video, Dr. Ashley Hendry's hospital cold call, holds **6,200,000 views, which is 66.1% of the 9,376,300 views in the whole library**. *Verified*. I also checked Parker chat history, 10 threads, all from 2026-09-03 and 2026-09-04. Nobody on this team has told Parker anything about how the brand should look. That is a real absence and it is why the visual side of this brain has been running on guesswork until now.

The reading standard through all of it is `visuals.md`: assume sound off, copy skimmed, captions unseen, and ask whether the frame carries the message alone. That is not a hypothetical here. **The account's biggest ad has no spoken word until 0:18.** For eighteen seconds it is a picture and a sentence, and it is the single largest line item in the business.

## Visual hooks

The visual hook gets the richest treatment because it is the most replayable thing the brand owns. Each one below is reconstructed as a scene, named against the hook taxonomy in `hooks.md` and the container in `ad-formats/`, then classified.

### 1. The silent bed. Signature. In play, verified.

A high-angle shot looking down at a bed made up in white. A woman in her late twenties or early thirties, light brown hair, in casual sleepwear, is lying on her side with an infant tucked against her. Both of them are looking straight up into the lens. The wall behind is soft beige. The camera does not move; it reads as a phone propped on a shelf or held above them. Across the middle of the frame, in large white sans-serif, center-aligned, sits: *"POV: Telling your husband you found better health insurance, saved $400 a month, AND the deductible is zero. Wife of the year energy. 💕"* An acoustic track is already mid-phrase when the video starts, male choral vocals singing *"These memories will stay with me."* Nobody speaks. At about 0:09 it cuts to the same mother on a carpeted living room floor playing with two toddlers, same text still sitting over it. The first line of dialogue does not land until **0:18**, and when it does, it is a different woman entirely: a blonde spokesperson in her forties or fifties in a green floral dress, in a bright living room with framed art and plants, saying *"Listen, this is why you can't simply just say, 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered.'"*

Ad `Moms43 - 4 - V3`, launched 2026-04-15. In the window it spent **$43,119.77**, which is **43.1% of all video spend in the account for those 90 days**, and returned **1,935 leads at a $22.28 cost per lead** on a **45.36% hook rate**, a **3.28% hold rate**, **83.73% first-frame retention** and a **4.04 second average play time**. *Verified* from period metrics and a frame-level read.

Hook format is POV in the `hooks.md` sense, and POV in `ad-formats/both/index.md` too, where the format is defined by the literal on-screen cue. But naming it POV undersells the mechanic. Read against `visuals.md`, this frame is doing three of the eight principles at once. Human presence, because two faces are looking directly down the lens. Emotional specificity, because the state is not generic happiness, it is a small private win. And cognitive ease, because the image is one plane, two subjects, no competing elements, so the eye has nothing to sort before it reads the words. The half-second test passes on the sentence, not the picture: the picture alone does not say health insurance.

This is the account's signature opener by a distance. Read across the library, the same line appears on **20 distinct video ads across the account's lifetime, carrying $87,991.09 and 4,499 leads at a $19.56 blended cost per lead**, per the September hook audit's two independent keyword pulls. *Verified* there. Four of those 20 were built inside the last window. So this is vocabulary, not a one-off.

### 2. The walk out of the house. Signature. In play, verified.

Same sentence, different picture, and the difference matters. Instead of a static bed, the frame opens on a montage cut every three seconds or so, under a whimsical flute-and-piano instrumental. A mother in a white halter top and beige skirt holds her son's hand walking out of a modern home. Cut to a mother and daughter in matching pink athletic wear taking a mirror selfie. Cut to a mother kneeling on a gravel path to talk to a toddler. The same white POV block sits across the top of all of it for the first ten seconds. At 0:10 the video hands off to a woman in her late twenties in a sleeveless camo-print Budweiser tank, dark hair tied back, in a bedroom with neutral walls and a white laundry basket visible behind her.

Ads `moms-63 2b` and `moms-63 3e`, both launched 2026-08-14. In the window, 2b spent **$5,529.74 for 417 leads at $13.26** on a **40.15% hook rate** and a **2.39% hold rate**; 3e spent **$5,448.56 for 353 leads at $15.44** on a **42.89% hook** and a **2.88% hold**. *Verified*. Those two cost-per-lead figures are the cheapest of any meaningful spender in the window.

Same hook format as the silent bed, different visual grammar underneath it. This is worth recording as its own entry rather than as a variant, because it proves the brand can carry its signature line on a moving montage as well as on a held frame, and the montage version happens to be the one it filmed most recently.

### 3. The stranger's post pasted over a face. Signature. In play, verified.

A woman films herself at arm's length, indoors, domestic light. Composited over the lower or middle third of the frame is a screenshot styled as a viral post from "Cindie Hayvern": *"My deductible is $6000. I have to pay $6000 before my insurance even kicks in...but yet...I pay $1000/mo for that insurance?! Make that make sense...."* She does not read it aloud. She talks over it: *"This is why you can't simply just say 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered.'"* The framing varies across the family and the overlay does not. One version has her in a dark hoodie standing in a home with natural light, `Moms43 - 5 - V1`, **$10,170.58 in the window, 429 leads at $23.71, 38.39% hook, 7.59% hold, a 2.14% CTR** and the highest CPM among the big spenders at **$31.02**. One has her in a grey hoodie lying on a couch, `MOMS39 - 2 - V2 - Copy`, **$1,472.06, 79 leads at $18.63, 36.93% hook, 5.01% hold, 2.44% CTR**. One has her standing in a modern interior wearing a gold "Mama" pendant, `moms54-2`, **$1,467.48, 42 leads at $34.94**. *Verified* on all three.

The container is Social Interface from `ad-formats/both/index.md`, defined by a recognisable platform interface appearing inside the creative. The hook format is closest to Comment Response in `hooks.md`, though it is not one, because the post is not a comment on the brand's own ad. The September hook audit named this mechanic a screenshot-outrage overlay and that is the right description. The reason it works is borrowed proof: someone else's grievance is on screen in frame zero, so the eye has something to read while the ear starts up, and the complaint feels found rather than written.

This family holds people roughly twice as well as the POV openers, at 5.01% to 7.59% against 2.39% to 3.28%. That is the clearest evidence in the account that putting a readable artifact in the frame buys watch time.

### 4. The wall of state names. Signature, and the most distinctive thing the brand owns. In play, verified.

A woman in her early thirties, dark hair tied back, a nose piercing, visible tattoos on both upper arms, in a grey rib-knit tank top. She is standing in front of a clean white screen that is filled edge to edge with a long list of US state names in two columns under a header reading **"Approved State List ✔️"**. The list is not an overlay here. It is the room. Before you process a word she has said, your eye is already hunting the list for your own state. Her first line is warm and immediate: *"I'm so thankful for the mom that told me about this."* At 0:02 she qualifies you out loud: *"If you're a mom and you live in one of these states and you're tired of paying high deductibles, high premiums for health insurance for your family, listen up."* She uses her hands constantly.

Ad `moms-53 3`, launched 2026-08-25. In the window it spent **$2,041.29 for 127 leads at a $16.07 cost per lead**, on a **35.49% hook rate**, a **3.15% CTR** and a **$0.69 cost per click**. That CTR is the highest of any ad in the window's top spenders and the CPC is by far the lowest. *Verified*.

The hook format is Conversational in `hooks.md`, and the container is Callout in `ad-formats/both/index.md`. But the format label misses the point, which `visuals.md` names precisely: this frame passes the half-second test on the *picture*, not the sentence. A wall of state names with checkmarks reads as an official register in half a second, and it invites a small act of participation almost nobody scrolls past. It is also the account's only genuine document, and documents are the recurring visual event across the whole category feed.

The brand has now shot this same artifact four ways, which is covered under per-format grammar below.

### 5. The crying close-up. Signature within its lane, recent, climbing. In play, verified.

A tight handheld close-up, so close you can see the whites of her eyes are red. A woman in her early twenties, fair skin, dark hair up, in a dark grey graphic t-shirt, filming herself in what looks like her own living room or bedroom in soft natural light. There is a slight instability to the frame that reads as a phone in one hand. **There is no music at all.** Her voice is already breaking: *"Just got off a two hour call fighting for them to cover my newborn son's hospital stay."* White sans-serif captions appear in the centre of the frame one phrase at a time and fade out, and the timing is exact: *"Just got off a two hour call"* at 0:00 to 0:01, *"to cover my newborn son's"* at 0:02 to 0:04, *"hospital stay."* at 0:04 to 0:05, *"Just zero"* at 0:05 to 0:07, *"humanity, zero compassion."* at 0:07 to 0:08. She wipes her face at 0:05. At 0:12 there is one hard cut to a completely different woman in a bright modern house with a staircase, a crystal chandelier and a potted plant behind her, wearing a tie-dye tank, gold hoops and a gold "Mama" necklace, delivering the pitch calmly.

Ad `moms54-3`, launched 2026-08-13. In the window it spent **$1,283.69 for 43 leads at a $29.85 cost per lead**, on a **57.02% hook rate**, a **13.66% hold rate**, an **8.08 second average play time**, **85.75% first-frame retention** and a **3.13% CTR**. *Verified* from period metrics and from a frame-level read of the video. Its sibling `moms54-4` opens the same way on a woman crying about a son who fell off the monkey bars: **52.40% hook, 8.62% hold, 17 leads at $38.09**.

This is the ad the diversity audit could not name. Its format tag is `Other`, the label applied when no defined format fits, and the audit inferred it was closest to `Yapper`. Having watched it, I would put it differently. Structurally it is a **Reaction hook** in the `hooks.md` sense, an emotional cliffhanger held for two beats before anything is explained, mounted on top of the account's standard talking-head body. What makes the tagger stumble is that the two halves belong to different production worlds: half one is unlit, unscored and unstable, half two is lit, composed and scripted. The account has no name for that because it has never treated the raw half as a format in its own right.

Two things about it are load-bearing for the vocabulary. First, this is the only creative in the account clearing the 12% hold floor at any real spend, and the only one crossing eight seconds of average play. Second, the silence is a choice that shows: no music at all in either half, which `hooks.md` counts as a sound hook rather than an absence of one.

### 6. The toddler and the drink. Incidental, one-off. In play, verified.

An extreme close-up of a small child in a beige fleece coat drinking from a cup. Animated pink bows are stuck onto her hair and a heart emoji floats nearby. There is no problem anywhere in this frame. No bill, no screenshot, nobody crying. Just a toddler having a drink and a line of text claiming peace: *"POV: At peace, because my family finally has the best health insurance 💗"*. Gentle acoustic music. At 0:12 it hands off to a spokesperson in her mid-forties in a grey marl sweater.

Ad `moms-66-816`, launched 2026-08-18. Lifetime **$1,152.04, 51 leads at $22.59, 40.27% hook rate, 1.92% hold rate**. *Verified*. Its sibling `moms-66-816 - 2` opens on a mother curled up with a baby on a big white sofa and ran the **highest hook rate in the account at 64.15%**, on **$138.86** and 4 leads, which is far too thin to call.

This is the purest permission hook in the account in the `hooks.md` sense: it releases rather than activates, it opens in the calm and positive zone, and it makes no demand of the viewer at all. Worth logging as vocabulary because it proves the brand can stop a scroll with an image that contains no conflict whatsoever. Worth flagging as incidental rather than signature because it has run twice and the hold rate says the opener writes a cheque the body does not cash.

### 7. The dancing boy. Incidental, one-off. In play, verified.

A young boy of about eight or ten in sunglasses and a tan sweatshirt performing a full exaggerated dance routine in front of a staircase in a modern house, to a remix of Soulja Boy's *Crank That*. A woman in a white sweatshirt jumps into frame and matches his moves. Text overlay: *"POV: You get the explanation of benefits and your insurance actually... covered it. All of it. This is what it's supposed to feel like. 💕"* The music drops out at 0:08 and the same mid-forties spokesperson takes over, with purple and white captions mirroring her words.

Ad `moms-66-816 - 5`. **52.71% hook rate on $47.38** and now paused. *Verified*. Too thin for any verdict, and I am recording it only because it is the account's single use of the Trends format from `ad-formats/video/index.md`, meaning a borrowed viral audio and a recognisable movement template. That is a real capability, filmed once.

### 8. The school pickup line. Incidental, recent. In play, verified.

A woman in her thirties in large orange-tinted sunglasses and a Budweiser muscle tank, filming herself from the driver's seat of her car in daylight. She looks like a person killing ten minutes, not like an ad. She starts mid-thought: *"Currently waiting in line to pick up my girls from school, and I'm doing the math on how much I pay for health insurance every single year, not to mention the huge deductible on top of that."* Overlay: *"Here's why Moms are cancelling their health plans 👇"*.

Ad `moms55-1`, launched 2026-08-28. In the window it spent **$688.07 for 32 leads at $21.50**, on a **43.52% hook rate**, a **4.05% hold rate** and **85.98% first-frame retention**. *Verified*.

Container is UGC Single. The hook is a Conversational opener stacked with a Controversy overlay that makes a claim the body then has to earn. What makes it worth its own entry is the setting, not the line. This is the only unit in the account filmed inside a real named mom moment rather than in a generic bright room, and the brand's own guidelines list the school pickup line among the things it wants to be associated with. *Stated*, brand context section 7 by way of `brand-identity-analysis.md`.

### 9. The porch phone call. Signature within its lane, dormant. In play, verified.

A woman in her thirties in a pink sweatshirt stands outside on a porch holding a phone to her ear, visibly annoyed. On-screen text: *"When you have to call your health insurance 🙄"*. She says *"I'm calling to see why this wasn't covered when y'all said it would be."* Three seconds later it cuts to the same actor playing the other end of the line, in a blue t-shirt and a headset, typing at a laptop in a living room, deadpanning *"Hmm it looks like it's not covered cuz it happened on a Tuesday."* The bit escalates: coverage only on Mondays, Wednesdays and federal holidays that fall on a Friday; *"For access to coverage, not necessarily coverage itself"*; *"You need to be declared clinically dead and revived twice."* A sibling version uses a red pencil as a makeshift microphone. At 0:54 the same actor, now in a beige sweater in a kitchen, drops the bit and pitches straight to camera.

Ads `MOMS38 - 2 - V3` and `MOMS38 - 2 - V4`, both launched 2026-03-11 in an ad set the team itself named "Moms38 - 2 - 1 (when you call your insurance)". V4 holds **27.31% of viewers on a 55.57% hook rate, $2,172.45 lifetime, 95 leads at $22.87 and a 4.56% CTR**. V3 holds **24.48% on a 51.78% hook, $1,823.59, 59 leads at $30.91**. *Verified*.

**Those are the two highest hold rates anywhere in this account, and it is not close.** The biggest spender holds 3.28%. The craft floor in `ad-account-analysis.md` is 12%. Read against `hooks.md`, the reason is named directly in the doc's four first-second mechanics: precognitive sound, familiar audio with half of it missing, one side of a phone call with an audible pause where the reply should be. The brand built that mechanic, proved it holds better than anything else it has ever made, and has not spent meaningfully on it since March.

### 10. The laptop shock. Incidental, dormant. In play, verified.

A blonde woman in a black t-shirt and glasses sits at a laptop, face contorted, scrolling fast. Text screams across the frame: *"What!? 😳"* then *"i owe a $20,000 bill for anesthesiology??"* She says it out loud. At 0:07 she is on the phone to the hospital. At 0:14 the same actor, now in a white shirt, plays the hospital rep and delivers the line casually: *"Ohh yeah, we had to use an out-of-network anesthesiologist for you, and they weren't covered by your insurance. Sorry about that!"*

Ad `Yeti State Angle - 2 - V1`, launched 2026-03-13. **$826.09 lifetime, 31 leads at $26.65, 42.47% hook rate, 12.55% hold rate**. *Verified*. Another unit clearing the hold floor, at trivial spend, dormant.

### 11. The claymation desk of bills. Incidental, new, dormant. In play, verified.

A narrator asks: *"Did you know your hospital bill is cheaper if you don't use your insurance?"* At 0:04 the style changes to clean 3D: a mother and her son in a bright hospital hallway beside an MRI machine, with a small green price tag and a much larger blue one floating above them. At 0:10 a cash icon and an insurance card icon each connect to a tag, and the insurance tag visibly grows. At 0:17 a tall office tower with a shield-and-umbrella logo looms over a small hospital and a pharmacy, glowing blue lines running between them. At 0:23 two identical twin women sit across a table from each other while a number between them inflates from $1.10 to over $59.50.

Ad `Moms46 - V1`, launched 2026-07-02. **$381.82, 12 leads at $31.82, 31.25% hook rate, 13.26% hold rate**. *Verified*.

This is the only creative in the account that stages an actual demonstration rather than asserting a claim, and it holds better than anything else in the window. It is also the account's only Graphic Video in the `ad-formats/video/index.md` sense, the whole frame built rather than filmed, no real people. It is not a format that failed. It is a format that was never put in market.

### 12. The pediatrician who never speaks. In play as a picture, unproven as a voice. Verified.

A brightly lit pediatric exam room. A female doctor in a white coat and a mask holds a stethoscope to the chest of a baby sitting in a diaper on crinkly exam-table paper. Bold text carries the whole message: *"Moms... Leave your big health insurance company for a mom-focused one. Save up to 30% and get $0 deductible options. 😊"* At 0:07 the baby laughs and kicks and the doctor smiles at her. At 0:11 a lavender end card. **The entire spoken content of the ad is one line at 0:11, from an unnamed narrator: *"Finally, insurance that actually feels like it has our back."*** The doctor never says a word.

Ad `MOMS30 - 1 - V20`, launched 2026-01-27. **$1,886.00 lifetime, 85 leads at $22.19, 39.91% hook rate, 1.81% hold rate**. *Verified*.

Container is Authority Figure in `ad-formats/both/index.md`, and it fails the format's own definition, because the format is about a credible expert whose credentials make the *explanation* carry weight, and there is no explanation. **1. Every ad in the account is a woman alone, and the two things that hold longest both put a second character in the frame.**
