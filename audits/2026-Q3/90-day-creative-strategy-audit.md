# 90-day creative strategy audit — Health For Moms — 2026-Q3

## Executive summary

This account has become one ad. In the ninety days from 5 June to 2 September 2026, Health For Moms spent $98,276.81 on Meta across 136 ads and brought in 4,336 leads at a $22.67 cost per lead. A single ad, `Moms43 - 4 - V3`, took $42,741.16 of that, or 43.5 percent of everything spent. The top three ads took 60.8 percent. The top ten took 88 percent and delivered 90.1 percent of the leads. Every claim in that paragraph is **verified** from the live account pull. Concentration this tight is not automatically wrong, and Meta's own delivery system pushed the money there for a reason. But it means the account's whole quarter rests on one creative idea, filmed four ways, and the audit has to read that idea very carefully.

The idea is the approved states list. In all ten of the top-spend ads, a woman tells you that moms in certain states can get a health plan with a zero dollar deductible, a full-screen graphic of state names appears, and she tells you to click and check in thirty seconds. That is an open loop the viewer cannot close in her head, so she has to click to close it, and the brand told us this is their proven lever. As stated in the brand context, "State angle has worked very well if you see the account." The account agrees. What the account also shows, and this is the part the brand may not have seen, is that the state list is now doing nearly all the persuading by itself. **Verified**, confidence strong.

The quarter is a scale-down, not a scale-up, and that reframes everything. The preceding ninety days, from 7 March to 4 June, ran 460 ads and $375,185.71 for 16,560 leads at a $22.66 cost per lead. Spend fell 73.8 percent quarter over quarter and lead volume fell by exactly the same 73.8 percent, while cost per lead moved four one-hundredths of one percent. Daily spend went from roughly $4,169 to roughly $1,092. **Verified** from two direct pulls. So efficiency held perfectly while the machine got three quarters smaller. The brand's stated goal is "SCALE BABY!" and its own grading scale calls a $22.67 cost per lead "meh." The account is not scaling and it is not hitting the number the brand wants.

The most consequential creative finding is a collapse in hold rate that nobody would spot from the cost per lead. Last quarter's six biggest ads together took 34.4 percent of spend and every one of them held 14 percent or more of viewers to the end. This quarter, not one ad in the top ten clears 8 percent. The best is 7.60 percent and six of the ten sit under 4.5 percent. The craft standard Parker grades against puts the floor at 12 percent. Meanwhile hook rate stayed strong, with seven of the top ten above 35 percent and four above 44 percent. The account still stops the scroll beautifully. It has almost entirely stopped holding anyone. **Verified**, confidence strong.

The most surprising movement of the quarter is emotional. Anger carried 55.2 percent of spend last quarter, driven by ads whose text hook was literally "Health Insurance is a scam 🙄." This quarter anger carries 2.6 percent. In its place sits pride at 49.5 percent, almost all of it from one text hook about telling your husband you saved $400 a month and earning "Wife of the year energy." The account swapped a lane that ran on shared outrage for one that runs on domestic self-congratulation, and it did so without any recorded decision. That swap, read against the hold rate collapse and against the comment section, is the throughline the big picture section resolves at the end. **Verified** for the numbers, **inferred** for the causal link, confidence mixed.

## Deep dive on each of the top 10

### 1. `Moms43 - 4 - V3` — $42,741.16, CPL $22.21, hook 45.36 percent, hold 3.27 percent, POV, Solution Aware

The ad opens on a young woman lying in bed under white covers, an infant tucked beside her, both looking softly toward the camera against a warm beige wall. No one speaks. An acoustic cover plays, the lyric "These memories will stay with me" drifting under the shot. Across the top of the frame a long block of text sits for the entire first stretch: "POV: Telling your husband you found better health insurance, saved $400 a month, AND the deductible is zero. Wife of the year energy. 💕" At nine seconds the ad cuts to the same mother on a carpeted living room floor, tickling two laughing toddlers, and the text stays put. Only at eighteen seconds does anyone speak. A blonde woman in her late forties or early fifties, in a green floral dress, leans slightly toward the lens and says: "Listen, this is why you can't simply just say, 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered.'" She continues, "No. You need health insurance that has a zero dollar deductible without spending thousands." Then the turn: "And you can only get that if you are a mom and you live in one of these states." At thirty-three seconds the screen fills with a grey card headed "Approved States ✅" listing dozens of state names, each with a green check. She returns to tell you a new website gets better rates, and closes with "It'll take you 30 seconds." A white and purple end card lands at forty-five seconds with the logo and the line "save up to 30%," and a younger voice says, "Finally, insurance that actually feels like it has our back."

The lever pulling the weight here is the geographic open loop, not the emotion. The first eighteen seconds carry no argument at all. They are a mood and a promise painted in text, and the hook rate of 45.36 percent says that mood absolutely stops the scroll. Then the persuasion arrives all at once, from a different woman, in a different room, seventeen seconds after the viewer arrived. The hold rate of 3.27 percent and the average play time of 4.04 seconds tell you what happens next: almost nobody is still watching when the argument starts. **Verified** from the pull, confidence strong. Read against the craft standard, this ad clears the hook bar of 30 percent by a wide margin and misses the hold floor of 12 percent by nearly four times.

So why is it the account's biggest spender by a factor of four? Because the text hook alone is a complete sales message. A viewer reads "saved $400 a month, AND the deductible is zero" in two seconds, forms the whole offer in her head, and clicks. The video never has to work. That is a real and durable mechanism, and it is worth naming plainly rather than treating the ad as a video ad that happens to have low retention. It is functionally a static ad with footage running behind it. The 1.75 percent click-through rate, the $1.24 cost per click and 21,085 link clicks all say the same thing: this creative converts attention into clicks efficiently and converts watch time into nothing.

The risk sits in what happens after the click. This ad produced 1,924 leads at $22.21, which lands in the brand's own "meh" band, and it also produced 66 of the 92 comments the account received this quarter, or 71.7 percent of them. Reading those comments is uncomfortable. Fifty of the account's 92 comments are a bare "Help" or "Info" with nothing else, and the great majority of those sit here. The substantive ones are mostly people finding out they do not qualify. One reads, "It only works if you don't have any preexisting conditions. You gotta be perfectly healthy to qualify it looks like." Another: "Cassie Smithson I didn't. I took the short survey twice and slightly changed up my answers and it still didn't work out for me. Bummer." A third, from someone identifying as a licensed health insurance advisor: "As a licensed health insurance advisor, never put your info online unless you want 100 calls a day about health insurance." **Verified** verbatims from the ad comment pull, all within the window.

What this implies for the next round is uncomfortable and useful. The ad is buying a click on a promise the funnel then has to walk back for a meaningful share of people. Because the text hook does all the selling, it also does all the over-promising, and the video body never gets watched long enough to qualify anyone. The obvious iteration is not a new hook. It is moving one qualifying line into the first three seconds, where it will actually be read, and finding out whether a slightly smaller, better-qualified click volume moves the cost per lead out of "meh."

Make that make sense...." She starts talking immediately, at zero seconds: "This is why you can't simply just say 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered.'" By five seconds she names the fix, by ten the approved state list appears, by fourteen she is telling you to click, and at twenty-four she lands the closing line, "Stop overpaying with Health for Moms and get coverage that actually works." A voiceover and end card wrap it at twenty-eight seconds.

This is the best-retaining ad at real scale in the account, and it is not close. Its 7.60 percent hold rate is more than double the top ad's 3.27 percent, and it is the only ad above $2,000 in spend that gets anywhere near the retention range. Its 2.14 percent click-through rate also beats the top ad's 1.75 percent. The reason is structural: it starts talking at second zero. There is no eighteen-second wait for the argument. The tweet overlay does the work the mood montage does in ad one, except it does it while the pitch is already running, so the viewer gets stopped and informed in the same breath. **Verified**, confidence strong.

The tweet is doing something the montage cannot. It is other people's anger, borrowed and displayed. The specific number, $6,000 against $1,000 a month, is the kind of odd, concrete figure the brand's own guidelines call for when they note that "specificity reads as credibility." And it is not the brand making the complaint, which matters in a category where the brand has a legitimacy problem. The comment section reflects that this landed as an argument rather than an ad. One viewer wrote back, "It doesn't make sense. It's never made sense. There's a whole bunch of people that justify it and back it." Another argued with it directly: "That's not true. Yeah insurance covers 100% once you hit deductible but they still cover stuff before that. And if its in network they cover alot. I had blood work done and my insurance covered 640 dollars of the 700 dollar test." People do not argue with a mood. **Verified** verbatims.

Set against the rest of the top ten, this ad is the account's quiet proof that the state list does not need the montage. It carries the same offer, the same approved states graphic, the same thirty-second promise, and it delivers a cost per lead within $1.37 of the top ad while holding more than twice the attention. Its weakness is cost: a $30.89 CPM against the top ad's $21.66, which is 43 percent more expensive per thousand impressions. That gap is the honest reason spend went elsewhere. It suggests the account is currently optimizing for cheap reach over engaged reach, and it raises a genuine question about which one produces a lead an agent can actually close.

### 3. `Moms43 - 4 - V1` — $6,993.31, CPL $26.90, hook 44.85 percent, hold 4.32 percent, POV, Solution Aware

The same bedroom, the same baby, the same piano cover, the same "Wife of the year energy" text block. Ten seconds in, the same living room floor and the same two laughing toddlers. The only real difference arrives at nineteen seconds, when the spokesperson changes. Here she is a woman in her mid-thirties to early forties with a short blonde bob and a denim jacket, sitting in a bright modern living room, and she reads a nearly identical script: the "you can't simply just say" line, the zero dollar deductible, the state gate, the approved states card at thirty-three seconds, the thirty-second promise, the same end card and the same "Finally, insurance that actually feels like it has our back."

This is a spokesperson swap on a proven body, which the iteration doctrine names as one of the highest-confidence moves available. It lands mid. Hold rate improves from 3.27 to 4.32 percent against version three, but cost per lead worsens from $22.21 to $26.90, crossing out of the brand's "meh" band into "not good." It has been paused. **Verified**. The read is that the denim jacket peer archetype does not carry the same authority as the floral-dress woman in her late forties, in a category where trust is the actual barrier. The craft note that authority earns credibility in trust-sensitive categories fits here, and the difference between the two women is roughly fifteen years of apparent age.

What this ad really shows is how narrow the account's iteration ladder has been. Versions one, three and four of `Moms43 - 4` are the same footage, the same text hook and the same script with a different woman delivering the middle. Together they took $56,095.00, or 57.1 percent of the entire quarter's spend. That is three ads, one idea, more than half the money. The signal-change ladder in the iteration doctrine would call a spokesperson swap a persona-level change, which is real, but it sits underneath format and vehicle, which are the loudest levers. The account pulled the quieter lever three times and never pulled the loud one.

### 4. `Moms43 - 4 - V4` — $6,360.53, CPL $30.43, hook 47.06 percent, hold 4.30 percent, POV

This ad carried no stored creative analysis and no AI tags, so its creative was read by running a fresh analysis on the stored media file. The result: it opens on a woman in her late twenties to mid thirties, light brown hair in a messy bun, lying in bed beside an infant in a neutral bedroom, then moves to a carpeted living room with a cream couch where she plays with two toddlers. The same "Wife of the year energy" text block holds from zero to seventeen seconds. At eighteen the ad switches to a tight selfie-style shot of a woman in a grey hoodie, with the same Cindie Hayvern tweet about the $6,000 deductible laid over her, and she delivers the familiar script in staged word-by-word overlays: "This is why," "I have insurance," "no," "health insurance that has a $0 deductible," "without spending thousands." The approved state list appears from thirty to thirty-three seconds, listing thirty states from Alabama to Wyoming. She closes with "stop overpaying with health for moms that actually works." A blue end card at forty-seven seconds carries the logo, "save up to 30%," and a full legal disclaimer stating that the story is a dramatization featuring a paid actor and that HealthForMoms is not an insurance company but connects individuals with licensed agents and carriers. **Verified** by direct video analysis, 2026-09-03.

This version has the highest hook rate in the entire top ten at 47.06 percent and the worst genuine cost per lead in the whole `Moms43 - 4` family at $30.43. That pairing is the single cleanest illustration in the account of a principle the craft docs state directly: a high hook rate tells you the ad is generating interest, not that it is generating money. Version four stops more people than version three and converts them worse by 37 percent. It has been paused, correctly.

The reason is probably the fusion. This ad staples the mood montage of version three onto the tweet overlay of `Moms43 - 5`, which is a Frankenstein stitch of two proven elements. But the stitch runs the wrong way round. The tweet is a problem-aware device that works when it lands in the first second, as it does in `Moms43 - 5 - V1` where hold rate reaches 7.60 percent. Buried at eighteen seconds behind a montage, it reaches almost nobody: hold rate here is 4.30 percent, barely different from the montage-only version three. The proven element was preserved and its mechanism was thrown away. That is a real lesson for the next stitch, and it is worth writing down.

It is also worth noting where the disclaimer sits. The legal copy that says "paid actor" and "not an insurance company" appears at forty-seven seconds, in an ad whose average viewer leaves at 4.08 seconds. Fewer than one in a hundred viewers ever sees it. That is not a compliance judgment, which is not this audit's job, but it is a structural fact about where this account puts its qualifying information, and it repeats across the top ten.

## Creative diversity — ad format

Start with the fact that governs everything else in this section. Of the 136 ads that spent money between 5 June and 2 September, 136 were video and zero were static. A direct pull filtered to static returns no rows and no matching ads. **Verified**. The brand told us its own preference is "We want to focus mostly on video ads. MAYBE some image ads are fine to find angles." The account has taken the first half of that sentence and ignored the second half entirely for a full quarter.

Format tags cover 110 labels across the 79 ads Parker has tagged, which is 58.1 percent of the ad count but 93.4 percent of the spend, so the spend-weighted read below is reliable and the count-weighted read is partial. On spend, POV takes 69.8 percent, or $68,631.26 across 24 tagged ads. Social Interface takes 15.8 percent, $15,511.42 across 23 ads. Callout takes 12.2 percent, $11,970.09 across 21 ads. Those three formats hold 97.8 percent of the quarter's tagged spend. Everything else is a rounding error: Mashup at 5.5 percent from a single ad, Comment Response at 3.0 percent across five, Other at 2.1 percent, Skit at 0.7 percent across six, UGC Single at 0.6 percent, Educational at 0.5 percent across seven, Graphic Video at 0.4 percent, AI Animation at 0.1 percent across four, and Wall of Text, Stitch Hooks, Green Screen and Podcast together at under $50 combined.

Here is where the count and the spend pull apart in a way that is itself the finding. POV carries 21.8 percent of the format labels and 69.8 percent of the spend. Educational carries 6.4 percent of the labels and 0.5 percent of the spend. Skit carries 5.5 percent of labels and 0.7 percent of spend. Stitch Hooks carries 4.5 percent of labels and one one-hundredth of one percent of spend, which is $8.11 across five ads. The account is running a reasonable spread of formats and funding exactly one of them. That is not a diversity problem in production. It is a diversity problem in delivery, and it means the team's testing work is not reaching a spend level where anything could be learned from it.

Against the prior ninety days the shift is dramatic and it happened without any recorded decision. POV went from 12.1 percent of spend to 69.8 percent, a nearly sixfold rise in share. Social Interface fell from 22.5 percent to 15.8 percent. Stitch Hooks fell from 8.9 percent to essentially zero. Skit fell from 8.1 percent to 0.7 percent. The catch-all Other format, which held the account's largest share last quarter at 36.9 percent across 29 ads, fell to 2.1 percent. And four formats that carried real money last quarter now carry none at all: Authority Figure at $8,884.74, Infomercial and VSL at $4,196.50, Humour at $3,284.03 and Street Interview at $296.58 have all gone to zero. **Verified** across both pulls.

The Authority Figure disappearance is the one worth flagging hardest. This is a category where, by the brand's own account, people search "is Health for Moms legit," where the platform is regulated and where the model is a referral to a third party the brand does not control. The hooks doctrine puts authority hooks squarely in trust-sensitive categories like health and finance, and calls them more expensive but worth it. The account spent $8,884.74 on four authority ads last quarter and zero this quarter. The brand's compliance rule is that a doctor character requires a real licensed physician or adjusted language, which is a real constraint but not a bar on authority in general. Licensed advisors, agents and the brand's own team are all authority sources that do not need a doctor. Whether the format was killed on performance or simply drifted out is something the account data cannot tell us, and it is one of the open loops below.

Format correlates with the account's metrics in a way that is consistent and not subtle. Every ad in the top ten built on the POV montage opens with between eleven and eighteen seconds of wordless footage, and every one of them lands a hold rate between 2.36 and 4.32 percent. The ads that put a person talking or a graphic in the first frame land higher: `Moms43 - 5 - V1` at 7.60 percent, and outside the top ten, `moms54-3` at 13.66 percent, `Moms46 - V1` at 13.59 percent, `Moms36 - 3 - A - 2 - V4c - Copy` at 11.16 percent and `Moms46 - V2` at 11.15 percent. Those four ads are the only ones in the entire quarter clearing a 10 percent hold rate, and together they took $2,407.89, which is 2.45 percent of the account. **Verified**, confidence strong. Two of those four are the claymation ads in the `Moms46` set, tagged Graphic Video and AI Animation, which the account funded with $381.82 and $86.06 and then paused.

What the mix is doing to the brand's range is straightforward. The account has one format that buys clicks cheaply and cannot hold a viewer, and a set of formats that hold viewers and have never been given enough money to prove anything. It has no static at all, so it has no way to test the same message in the loudest available signal change. It has no authority format in a trust-broken category. It has no humour, which the brand's own voice guidelines explicitly invite when they say "light humor about mom life chaos is welcome." And it has 22 video formats on the brand's own stated testing wish list, from ASMR to street interviews to us-versus-them, of which the quarter touched a handful at token spend. The whitespace here is not a subtle gap. It is most of the map.

## Emotions

Emotion tags cover the same 79 ads holding 93.4 percent of spend, with 152 emotion labels across them, an average of 1.92 emotions per ad. Read on spend, the quarter has one dominant feeling and it is not one the brand named as a goal. Pride carries 49.5 percent of spend, $48,691.94, across just six tagged ads. Hope carries 38.1 percent, $37,435.89, across 62 ads. Frustration carries 19.2 percent, $18,846.50, across 27. Tenderness carries 13.8 percent from four ads. Then a long drop: Empowerment at 6.2 percent, Relief at 5.5 percent, Anger at 2.6 percent, Fear at 2.2 percent, and Anxiety, Curiosity, Joy, Belonging, Contentment and Skepticism together at under $400 combined. The shares sum above 100 because ads carry more than one tag.

The gap between count and spend inside these numbers is the story. Pride sits on six ads and half the money. Hope sits on 62 ads and just over a third. That means pride is not a broadly tested emotion that won. It is a single creative idea that got funded. Trace it: `Moms43 - 4 - V3` at $42,741.16 is tagged Pride, and it is 87.8 percent of all pride spend in the quarter. The emotion the account is running on is one text hook, "Wife of the year energy," on one ad. **Verified**, confidence strong.

Hope is the account's real workhorse by breadth, and it is worth being honest about what hope is doing here. In these ads hope is not an emotion the creative generates. It is the shape of the offer. A zero dollar deductible, better rates, thirty seconds, save up to 30 percent. Every ad in the account ends there, so nearly every ad gets tagged Hope. It is closer to a structural feature of the CTA than to an emotional choice a strategist made. That is why 62 ads carry it and it still only holds 38.1 percent of spend.

Now the trajectory, and this is the largest single movement in the entire audit. Last quarter anger carried 55.2 percent of spend, $206,978.24 across 83 tagged ads. This quarter it carries 2.6 percent, $2,595.00 across 22 ads. That is a 98.7 percent fall in dollars behind the emotion. The ads that carried it are named and gone. `MOMS38 - 1 - V1`, text hook "Health Insurance is a scam 🙄," verbal hook "My deductible is $6,000," spent $54,173.20 last quarter at a $20.15 cost per lead with a 3.17 percent click-through rate and a 17.29 percent hold rate. Its siblings V2, V3, V9 and V5 spent another $66,213.37 between them, all at hold rates between 14.61 and 16.66 percent. A direct pull of those five ad IDs against the current window returns zero rows. They have no spend at all this quarter. **Verified** from both pulls.

That matters more than a tag change. The scam hook is a named format in the craft taxonomy, and the doctrine calls "scam" one of the most powerful trigger words in advertising right now, especially in categories with trust problems and lots of competing false promises. Health insurance lead generation is exactly that category. The brand's own compliance rules make the system the villain rather than any named company, which is precisely what a scam hook aimed at the category does. So the account abandoned a format that was legal for it, native to its category, and delivering a $20.15 cost per lead at a hold rate five times what anything in the current top ten manages. **Verified** for the numbers, **inferred** for the reading that the abandonment was drift rather than a decision, confidence mixed, because nothing in the chat history or the brand context records a call to kill it.

What replaced anger is the cluster of pride, tenderness and relief, which together carry 68.8 percent of this quarter's spend against 17.7 percent last quarter. These are all warm, low-intensity, positive feelings. Read through the emotional delivery lens, the account has moved almost entirely into the Soft zone: positive valence, low intensity. That zone is genuinely underused by most brands and it does real work at mid-funnel, where identification matters more than urgency. But it is being deployed here as a cold-traffic opener on the account's biggest ads, and the hold rate says it is not converting attention into anything. The viewer is stopped by a warm feeling and then leaves before an argument arrives. Anger did the opposite: it opened a fight the viewer wanted to see finish, which is why those ads held 17 percent.

Which emotions are missing is as loud as which are present. There is no amusement at all this quarter, down from $6,076.27 last quarter, in a brand whose own voice guide says light humour about mom life chaos is welcome. There is no trust as a tagged emotion, at $494.39 last quarter and zero now, in a category where "is this legit" is a documented search behaviour and where the comment section this quarter contains "don't believe this," "Not recommend" twice, and a licensed advisor warning people off. There is no defiance, at $1,823.59 last quarter and zero now. Curiosity carries $148.02 across three ads, which is 0.15 percent of spend, in an account whose single proven mechanism is a curiosity gap. That last one is close to funny. The state list works because it opens a loop, and the account has almost no spend tagged to curiosity, because the loop is being carried by a device rather than by an emotion the creative deliberately builds.

Mapped against what is actually working in section three, the emotional read gets sharper. The two best cost per lead figures in the top ten belong to `moms-63 2b` at $13.29, tagged Hope and Pride, and `moms-63 3e` at $15.14, tagged Empowerment. Empowerment carries only 6.2 percent of quarter spend and produced one of the two best numbers in it. The worst genuine cost per lead in the top ten belongs to `moms54-2` at $34.94, the account's only Fear-tagged ad in the group. Small samples, so confidence is mixed. But the direction is consistent: the account does better when it makes a mom feel capable and worse when it makes her feel threatened, and it has funded neither of those reads deliberately.

## Desires — Life Force 8

Drew Whitman's Life Force 8 names the eight desires humans are wired for before any advertiser gets to them: survival and enjoyment of life and life extension; enjoyment of food and beverages; freedom from fear, pain and danger; sexual companionship; comfortable living conditions; to be superior and winning and keeping up with others; care and protection of loved ones; and social approval. Health For Moms this quarter is running on one of them.

Care and protection of loved ones carries 86.5 percent of spend, $85,054.31 across 65 tagged ads. Social approval carries 5.3 percent, $5,165.76 across five ads. Freedom from fear, pain and danger carries 1.5 percent, $1,506.26 across six ads. Comfortable living conditions carries 33 cents on one ad. Two ads are tagged to no desire at all. Four of the eight desires are entirely untouched: survival and life extension, food and beverages, sexual companionship, and to be superior and winning. Denominator again is the 79 tagged ads holding 93.4 percent of spend. **Verified**.

Care and protection is the right lead for this brand and nobody should argue otherwise. The product is health coverage for a family, the audience is 95.2 percent female and 81.2 percent aged 25 to 44, and the brand's stated emotional promise is that mothers gain peace of mind knowing their family is protected. The desire and the product are honestly matched. The question is not whether it should lead. It is whether it should be 86.5 percent of everything, which is a level of concentration that stops being focus and starts being the only thing the account knows how to say.

The movement since last quarter tells you a real thing happened. Freedom from fear, pain and danger carried 29.2 percent of spend last quarter, $109,552.08 across 28 ads. It now carries 1.5 percent. That is a 98.6 percent collapse in dollars, and it is the desire-level shadow of the anger collapse in the emotions section. Those are the same ads. The scam hook ads were selling freedom from a specific danger, which is going broke because you believed you were covered. When the account stopped running them, it did not just lose an emotion. It lost an entire desire, and with it the only lane in the account that was not about protecting someone else. Care and protection rose from 63.4 percent to 86.5 percent to fill the hole. **Verified**.

Social approval is the interesting arrival. It carried zero spend last quarter and $5,165.76 this quarter, almost all of it on `moms-63 3e`, the ad with the second-best cost per lead in the top ten at $15.14. Look at where the account's social approval language actually lives and it is all in the text hooks, not the scripts: "Wife of the year energy," and on the sibling `moms-63 1f`, "POV: You shared Health for Moms in the group chat and now three friends have better insurance because of you. Legend behavior.💕" That is status among other mothers, which is a genuinely different engine from protecting a child. It has been tested at 5.3 percent of spend and it produced a "good" tier number on its first real outing. **Verified** for the numbers, **inferred** for the read, confidence mixed at 20 days of data.

The whitespace is the part the brand should sit with. Survival, enjoyment of life and life extension is untouched at zero dollars, and this is health insurance. The product's whole promise is that you can get care when you need it, which is the most literal possible expression of that desire, and the account has never once run at it. Every ad in the top ten sells savings and a deductible number. Not one sells being able to see a doctor. The comment "why don't any of the plans offer prenatal care?" and the question "Can I get it for just the kids" both come from women asking about care, not price. **Verified** verbatims from the window.

To be superior and winning is also untouched at zero, at $122.40 last quarter. That is a defensible choice given the brand's guardrail against punching down at demographic groups and its rule that the villain is always the system. Status competition between mothers is a live wire in this category and the brand has good reason to be careful with it. But the social approval lane it just opened sits right next to that line, so the choice deserves to be a deliberate one rather than an unexamined one. Food and beverages and sexual companionship being empty is simply correct for this product and needs no more discussion than that.

What the concentration reveals about the brand's assumptions is this. Running 86.5 percent of spend on care and protection assumes the mother's dominant motivation is her family's safety. The brand's own ICP work says something slightly different. Danielle is described as afraid of dropping one of the plates she is holding. Marissa wants to stop feeling like her family's safety depends on nothing going wrong. Courtney buys things when she feels in control after proving to herself that the life she built is stable. Those are all descriptions of a woman wanting relief from her own load, not only protection for her children. Relief carries 5.5 percent of this quarter's spend. The desire the account leads with is aimed at the children. The desire the personas describe is aimed at the mother. That gap is the audit's clearest unexploited lane, and it is **inferred** from the brand's stated ICPs rather than verified from buyer data, because no buyer data exists.

## Stage of awareness

Eugene Schwartz's five stages run from unaware, where the buyer does not know she has a problem, through problem aware, solution aware, product aware, and most aware. The account this quarter sits almost entirely in the middle two and is very lopsided inside them.

On spend, Solution Aware carries 56.2 percent, $55,274.33, across seven tagged ads. Problem Aware carries 37.1 percent, $36,446.90, across 71 ads. Unaware carries $36.35 on a single ad, which is four one-hundredths of one percent. Product Aware and Most Aware carry zero dollars and zero ads. Denominator is the 79 tagged ads holding 93.4 percent of spend. **Verified**.

The split between count and spend here is the most extreme in the whole audit and it needs saying plainly. Seven ads hold 56.2 percent of the money at Solution Aware. Seventy-one ads hold 37.1 percent at Problem Aware. That is a ratio of roughly $7,896 per solution-aware ad against $513 per problem-aware ad, a fifteen-fold difference in funding per ad. And the solution-aware money is almost entirely three ads: `Moms43 - 4 - V3` at $42,741.16, `Moms43 - 4 - V1` at $6,993.31 and `MOMS34 - N1 - 3a` at $5,359.15 together account for $55,093.62 of the $55,274.33. The account's solution-aware position is three ads, and two of the three are the same footage. **Verified**.

That matters because of what solution-aware creative is supposed to do. The craft standard says a solution-aware viewer knows she has a problem and knows solutions exist, and the right lead is problem-solution: name the problem, go straight to the solution. Look at what the account's solution-aware money is actually buying. `Moms43 - 4 - V3` spends its first eighteen seconds on a bedroom and a text overlay. `MOMS34 - N1 - 3a` spends its entire thirty-one seconds on three mothers and never states a problem or a mechanism at all. These are not problem-solution ads. They are warm identity ads that have been placed at the solution-aware stage, which explains why they hold 3.27 and 2.36 percent of viewers respectively. The tag says solution aware and the creative behaves most-aware, as if the viewer already knows and likes the brand. She does not. There is no purchase event in this account, no meaningful returning audience, and 4,336 leads against a claimed 5,000 moms served all time. **Inferred**, confidence mixed.

The trajectory here is a straight inversion. Last quarter Problem Aware carried 80.2 percent of spend, $300,770.33 across 142 ads, and Solution Aware carried 12.8 percent, $48,128.82 across 22. This quarter those shares are 37.1 percent and 56.2 percent. The account flipped its funnel posture in one quarter. **Verified**. And the ads that carried the old posture are the ones already named twice in this audit: the scam hook set, which opened by naming the problem in the first frame and held 14 to 17 percent of viewers. Problem awareness and hold rate travelled together, and they left together.

The unaware end is effectively empty and the brand's business stage says it should not be. The brand's stated goal is "SCALE BABY!" and its own read on itself is that it has "not a ton of creative diversity" and has been running mainly state angle iterations. A brand trying to grow spend from roughly $1,092 a day back toward the $4,169 a day it ran last quarter needs a bigger pool than problem-aware moms already frustrated with their deductible. The craft standard is direct about this: as you slide toward unaware the pool gets larger and the conversion gets harder, and living in the warm stages caps how many people you can reach. Thirty-six dollars and thirty-five cents of unaware spend in a quarter is not a test. It is an absence.

The other absence is different in kind and needs care. Product Aware and Most Aware sit at zero, and for an ecommerce brand that would be a clear finding about neglected repeat purchase. For a lead generation business with no purchase event, no repeat buyer and a handoff to a partner agency, most-aware creative has much less to do. There is no returning customer to remind. So the zero at the bottom of the funnel is largely appropriate and should not be flagged as a hole. The zero at the top of the funnel is the real one. **Inferred**, confidence strong on the reasoning, and it rests on the business model as described in the brand context rather than on account data.

Where the imbalance creates drag is at the handoff between stages inside a single ad. Every top-ten ad tries to move the viewer from problem to solution to click in under forty-five seconds, and the average viewer leaves at 3.2 to 4.4 seconds. So the awareness journey the creative is built to run almost never runs. The viewer sees a stage-appropriate first frame and never sees the rest. That is why the account's cost per lead is stubbornly flat at $22.67 across two very different quarters with two very different awareness mixes. The awareness stage of the body is not reaching enough people to move the number. Only the first three seconds are doing work, and the first three seconds are the same in nine of the top ten ads.

## Big picture

Hold every section in view at once and a single throughline appears that no section shows on its own. The account has stopped making ads and started making click-buying devices, and the reason it cannot tell is that its north-star metric cannot see the difference.

Here is the chain. Cost per lead held perfectly steady across two quarters, $22.66 then $22.67, while nearly everything underneath it changed. Spend fell 73.8 percent. Ad count fell 70.4 percent. The dominant format went from Social Interface and a long tail to POV at 69.8 percent. The dominant emotion went from anger at 55.2 percent to pride at 49.5 percent. The dominant awareness stage flipped from problem aware at 80.2 percent to solution aware at 56.2 percent. A whole Life Force desire, freedom from fear, pain and danger, fell 98.6 percent. Four formats went to zero. And the number the brand steers by did not move by more than a rounding error. **Verified**, all of it, from two direct pulls.

A flat cost per lead through that much upheaval is not a sign of stability. It is a sign that the metric is measuring something the creative barely influences. What actually moved is the thing cost per lead does not price: hold rate went from six ads at 14 to 17 percent carrying a third of the account, to zero ads above 8 percent anywhere in the top ten. Click-through rate fell 26.2 percent, from 2.44 to 1.80. Cost per link click rose 12 percent, from $1.75 to $1.96. The account is paying more for each click and the clicks are coming from people who have watched four seconds of a mood instead of fifteen seconds of an argument. Cost per lead cannot see that, because a lead is a form fill, and a form fill can be produced by a promise the ad never had to justify.

This is where the comment section stops being colour and becomes the load-bearing evidence. Ninety-two comments landed in the window, and 66 of them, 71.7 percent, sit on the single top-spend ad. Fifty of the 92 are a bare "Help" or "Info" with nothing else attached. Four are blank. That leaves 38 substantive comments, and the largest cluster inside them, ten of the 38, is people discovering they do not qualify: "It only works if you don't have any preexisting conditions," "Preexisting health condition of cancer disqualified me," "But if you make under 30k a year you don't qualify smh," "You just have to use all your tax credits and not be eligible for health insurance from your job," "I took the short survey twice and slightly changed up my answers and it still didn't work out for me. Bummer." Four more are people locked out by geography after the state card promised them: "Why is this being shown in IL if it's not on the list???😭🤦🏼‍♀️", "Ofc Georgia ain't on there 🤣", "What a joke!! They don't have quote for me In tx." Eight are legitimacy doubts, including "don't believe this," "Not recommend" twice, "Is this insurance or something like a Christian health share?" and a licensed advisor telling people "never put your info online unless you want 100 calls a day about health insurance." **Verified** verbatims, every one from the window.

Put those two things side by side and the picture resolves. The account's dominant creative sells a promise in the first two seconds of on-screen text, gets the click before any qualifying information is spoken, and hands a lead to a partner agency who then has to be the one to say no. The ad never had to do the qualifying because nobody watched long enough for it to try. The disclaimer that says "paid actor" and "not an insurance company" appears at forty-seven seconds in an ad whose average viewer leaves at 4.04. The state list, which is the one qualifier the creative does carry, appears between eleven and thirty-three seconds in nine of the top ten. The one ad that puts the state list in the first frame, `moms-53 3`, has the highest click-through rate in the top ten at 3.15 percent and the cheapest click at $0.67. Qualifying early did not cost this account volume. It bought better volume.

The second cross-sectional pattern is quieter and it concerns what the account did to its own best asset. Last quarter's scam hook ads were doing four things at once that the current creative does none of. They opened on the problem in the first frame, which is why they held. They ran on anger at the system, which is the one emotion the brand's compliance rules explicitly permit since the villain is the system rather than a company. They sold freedom from a specific danger, a whole Life Force desire the account has now abandoned. And they sat at problem aware, where the pool is wider. All four of those things vanished together, in one quarter, with no recorded decision anywhere in the brand context or the chat history. The iteration doctrine has a phrase for what happened next: do not over-iterate into sameness. Three versions of one ad now hold 57.1 percent of the quarter's spend.

There is a third pattern underneath both, and it is the one that would be easiest to miss. The account's format mix is masking a persona problem. The brand names five ICPs and four of them are not comfortable. Marissa lives in a two-bedroom apartment with a cracked windshield and a $900 emergency fund. Courtney did the math six times on a $480 premium. Nicole is on maternity leave getting three different answers from HR. Danielle is afraid of dropping a plate. The account's top-spend footage shows a mother in matching pink athletic wear outside a modern house with a large black door, and the comment "I'm sure the mom that can afford matching outfits prob just pays in cash 🤦‍♀️" is the only voice in the window responding to that footage directly. Meanwhile the delivery data says 95.2 percent female, 42.6 percent aged 35 to 44 and 38.6 percent aged 25 to 34, which matches the personas on demographics. The mismatch is not who the platform is reaching. It is what the creative is showing them about who this product is for. **Inferred**, confidence mixed, resting on one comment and the brand's own ICP descriptions rather than on buyer data, which does not exist.

Two more absences deserve naming because they are the kind that only show up when the whole picture is held at once. First, this account is in the health insurance category, where the brand's own record says people search whether it is legitimate, and it ran zero authority format spend this quarter after $8,884.74 last quarter. A category with a trust problem that runs no authority creative is choosing to solve trust with volume instead of credibility. Second, the brand's own calendar names Open Enrollment on 1 November as the single most important date of its year, and its own stated planning window is two months, which means creative for it should be in production right now. Nothing in the current quarter's top ten is built for that window. Everything is evergreen, tagged as such on 79 of 79 tagged ads. **Verified**. The audit is being written on 3 September and the ramp should already have started.

If the senior strategist's instinct gets to name one swing, it is this. If Health For Moms puts a qualifying line and the state list into the first three seconds for the mother who already has employer coverage and does not know it can be beaten, then cost per lead should hold or fall while the quality of the leads reaching agents rises, and hold rate should clear 8 percent for the first time this quarter. The persona is the working or partnered mother on an employer plan with a deductible above $5,000, who is the exact person `moms-63 3e` opens by voicing when she says "I already have health insurance, this doesn't apply to me." The messaging direction is early qualification as a trust move rather than a volume cost. The success signal is a hold rate above 8 percent at a cost per lead at or below $22.67 with a lower share of "I don't qualify" comments. That is one swing, it is testable inside a month, and it uses two things the account has already proven separately and never combined.

## Open loops

**1. What is actually stopping the leads this account already buys from becoming customers?**

The observation: the account produced 4,336 leads at $22.67 this quarter and 16,560 at $22.66 last quarter, and the cost per lead did not move even though the format, emotion, awareness and desire mix all changed completely. Meanwhile the comment section's largest substantive cluster, ten of 38 real comments, is people finding out they do not qualify after clicking, on grounds the ads never mention: pre-existing conditions, income under $30,000, having an employer plan available, and pregnancy.

The pull is **tension**. It fired when the flat cost per lead across two structurally different quarters was set beside a comment section full of people saying they were disqualified after they clicked. Both cannot be a full account of what the ads are doing.

The question: **What happens to a Health For Moms lead after it reaches the partner agency?**

Why it is a loop: cost per lead is the brand's north star and it appears to be nearly insensitive to creative. If a meaningful share of leads is being disqualified downstream, then the metric the whole account steers by is rewarding creative that produces unqualified clicks, and every creative decision made this quarter was optimised against the wrong signal. Answering it changes what "good" means for every brief from here on. Only the brand can answer this one, because the data sits with the partner agencies.

Territory: **Product**

**2. Why did the anger lane get switched off?**

The observation: last quarter, five ads led by `MOMS38 - 1 - V1` with the text hook "Health Insurance is a scam 🙄" spent $120,386.57 between them at cost per lead figures from $20.03 to $25.48 and hold rates from 14.61 to 17.29 percent. This quarter all five have exactly zero spend, anger fell from 55.2 percent of spend to 2.6 percent, and no ad anywhere in the current quarter holds above 7.60 percent. Nothing in the brand context or the four Parker chat threads records a decision to stop.

The pull is **surprise**. It fired when a direct pull of those five ad IDs against the current window came back with zero rows, after the same IDs had carried a third of the previous quarter's spend at the best retention numbers in the account's history.

The question: **What made the team move away from the scam-angle creative?**

Why it is a loop: if it was killed for a reason Parker cannot see, such as a platform warning, an agent complaint about lead quality, or a brand-safety call, then that reason is a hard constraint that should shape every future concept and Parker is currently blind to it. If it simply drifted out while newer ads were built, then the account has an unclaimed asset with the best hold rates it has ever produced sitting idle three months before Open Enrollment. Those two worlds call for opposite plans. Only the brand can answer this.

Territory: **Messaging**

**3. What does the mother who has employer coverage believe about switching?**

The observation: `moms-63 3e` is the only ad in the top ten that opens by voicing the objection "I already have health insurance, this doesn't apply to me," and it produced the second-best cost per lead in the group at $15.14 on the cheapest CPM at $18.60. The same belief shows up unprompted in the comments as "I already have a $0 deductible and have free insurance from my job! I'm covered!" and "But how? If your job offers insurance you have to pick an insurance. They make you pay for one." The account has run this objection once, at 4.7 percent of spend.

The pull is **pattern**. It fired when the same sentence turned up as the winning script's opening line and as a viewer's rebuttal in the comments on a different ad, within the same ninety days.

The question: **How do mothers on employer plans describe the reason they have never looked at anything else?**

Why it is a loop: this is the largest audience the brand is not speaking to, and the brand's own competitive read names employer coverage as its most common alternative. If Parker knew the actual belief holding these women in place, whether it is the tax credit, the payroll deduction, the assumption that employer plans are cheaper, or simply never having been given a reason to look, the messaging lane would be obvious and the top of the funnel would open up. Right now the account has one accidental data point and no understanding underneath it.

Territory: **Personas**

**4. Who else could carry credibility on camera for this brand?**

The observation: the account spent $8,884.74 on Authority Figure creative last quarter and zero this quarter. Every one of the ten biggest ads this quarter is delivered by a woman in her late twenties to early fifties presenting as a peer mom in a home setting. In the same window the comment section produced "don't believe this," "Not recommend" twice, "Is this insurance or something like a Christian health share?" and a real licensed health insurance advisor publicly telling viewers not to submit their information.

The pull is **gap**. It fired when the format distribution showed authority creative at exactly zero dollars in a category where the brand's own context notes people searching whether the brand is legitimate.

The question: **Who do mothers actually trust to tell them the truth about health insurance?**

Why it is a loop: the brand's compliance rule blocks a fake doctor but not authority itself, and licensed agents, the brand's own team, and other credible voices are all available. If the trusted voice is a licensed advisor, the fix is casting. If it is another mother who has already been through the process, the current casting is right and the problem is proof rather than person. Parker cannot currently tell which, and the answer decides where the next production budget goes.

Territory: **Creators and talent**
