# Monthly hook audit — Health For Moms — 2026-09

## Scope and how this was read

This is the baseline cycle. There is no prior hook audit to carry forward, so every usage count here is a first reading rather than an update, and nothing is marked "died on the feed" yet because there is no earlier snapshot to compare against.

The window is 2026-08-04 to 2026-09-02, which is what Parker's rolling 30-day preset returns, read in America/New_York. Inside that window the account ran **102 video ads** carrying **$45,974.78** in spend, **1,944,921 impressions**, **2,080 leads** at a **$22.10 CPL**, a **$23.64 CPM** and a **1.93% CTR**. Statics were filtered out at ingestion, as this doc type requires. I pulled the top 20 video ads by spend, which together carry **$45,593.03**, or **99.2% of all video spend in the window**. So this is not a sample. It is very close to the whole paid picture, and the hooks below are effectively the account's entire live opener set. *Verified* from `search_facebook_ads_sql`.

I read transcripts, not names. Parker carries a timestamped script for every paid video here, and I pulled nine of them in full so the first seconds could be reconstructed from what is actually said and shown rather than from ad names like "moms-63 2b". Every brand hook below rests on the text hook, visual hook, verbal hook and script fields together. Where a hook is carried by the visual and the overlay with no spoken line at all, I say so, because that turned out to be the single most important structural fact about the account's best ad.

Two things shape how I graded. First, this account is lead generation. There are zero purchases and ROAS is 0 everywhere, so it is meaningless here. The team gave Parker its own CPL tiers, and I judged against those: **under $15 is fantastic, $15 to $20 is good, $20 to $25 is meh, over $25 is not good**. *Stated* by the brand in the brand context document. Second, the hook standard in this doc is spend plus hook rate, per the prompt. The craft bar from `ad-account-analysis.md` is a hook rate above 30%, with 45% to 50% being strong, and a hold rate of 12% or better.

The audience is sharply drawn and worth holding in mind while reading every hook: **94.9% female**, **43.8% aged 35-44** and **35.7% aged 25-34**, so roughly four in five buyers sit between 25 and 44. **99.4% mobile**, **58.3% Facebook** and **41.4% Instagram**. *Verified* from the window's demographic split.

The external rings of this audit are mostly dark, and I want that stated plainly rather than buried. There are no tracked competitors, no affinity set and no inspo set. Those sections below carry the blank honestly instead of a guess. The one live external surface is the Parker TikTok mining library, which is where the niche organic section comes from.

## Brand hooks in use

The headline structural finding first, because it reframes everything below. **The account's best hook has no spoken words in it.** In the top spender, the first line of dialogue does not arrive until 0:18. Everything before that is a visual, a piece of music and a block of on-screen text. That is not a quirk, it is the mechanic doing the work, and it lines up exactly with what `hooks.md` says about the first second being won by the visual and sound hook while the spoken line does its job later at the re-hook. *Verified* from the transcript.

### 1. "POV: Telling your husband you found better health insurance, saved $400 a month, AND the deductible is zero. Wife of the year energy. 💕"

A woman and her baby are lying in bed together, propped on white bedding against a soft beige wall, both looking straight into the lens. Nobody speaks. A song is already playing, mid-phrase, and the words that come up over the shot are a small piece of theater: you are watching her imagine telling her husband she fixed something. The scene is quiet, warm, slightly staged in the way a nice family photo is staged. The first spoken line does not land until 0:18, and when it does it is the state pitch: "Listen, this is why you can't simply just say, 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered.'"

This is the account's workhorse and the single biggest line item in it. Ad "Moms43 - 4 - V3", launched **2026-04-15**, spent **$15,109.87 in the window** for **618 leads at a $24.45 CPL**, on a **45.55% hook rate** and a **3.30% hold rate**. Lifetime it has spent **$55,794.26** for **2,549 leads at $21.89 CPL**, holding a **45.76% hook rate**. *Verified*.

Usage count: this exact hook line, matched on both "Telling your husband" and "Wife of the year energy", appears on **20 distinct video ads** across the account's lifetime. Those 20 carry **$87,991.09** in spend and **4,499 leads at a $19.56 blended CPL**. *Verified* from two independent keyword pulls that returned the same count of 20. Trajectory: still climbing. Four of the 20 were created inside this window alone, on 2026-08-14, 2026-08-27 and two on 2026-08-31.

Why it works, in mechanism terms: this is a POV hook in the `hooks.md` sense, but the thing actually stopping the scroll is salience plus self-reference. A human face, a baby, and gentle motion win the involuntary glance, and then the overlay names her exact identity and her exact win. It also sits squarely in what `hook-psychology.md` calls Zone 1, calm and positive and low intensity, which is the zone that meets a scrolling mother in the mood she is already in rather than ambushing her. Awareness stage reads as **Solution Aware**: it assumes she already knows better plans exist and sells her the feeling of having found one. *Inferred* from the copy posture, per the awareness-tagging rule that copy is the primary signal.

The honest weakness is the hold. A 3.30% hold rate is far under the 12% floor, and average play time is 4.06 seconds. Almost everyone leaves before the state pitch is even spoken. It still produces leads because the click is fast and does not depend on the watch, but this hook wins job one and loses job two.

### 2. "if you're a mom and your deductible is insane listen to this"

Tight on a woman's face, close enough to see her eyes are wet. She is not performing distress so much as sitting in it. Laid over her is a screenshot of a post complaining about a $6,000 deductible, so the viewer is reading someone else's outrage across a stranger's crying face. Her voice comes in immediately and matches the frame: "If you're a mom and your deductible is insane, listen to this." Then it pivots fast to the argument at 0:04, "Can we stop acting like any health insurance is good health insurance?"

This is a Demographic hook stacked onto a Myth-busting turn, and it is the second-largest hook family in the account. Usage count: **22 distinct video ads** lifetime, carrying **$27,980.64** and **1,156 leads at a $24.20 CPL**. *Verified*.

The original, "Moms Nahuel WV#1 - V9", launched **2026-04-06** and has spent **$17,723.91** lifetime for **635 leads at a $27.91 CPL**, on a **26.95% hook rate** and a 5.58% hold rate. *Verified*. That hook rate sits below the 30% bar and well below the 45.76% the POV hook holds, and it has had plenty of spend to prove itself. It is the strongest true weak-hook candidate in the account, and it is treated as such in the next section.

Trajectory: climbing in count but the copies are not beating the original. The two copies running in this window landed at 29.43% and 25.16% hook rates, which is flat to slightly worse.

### 3. "My deductible is $6000. I have to pay $6000 before my insurance even kicks in...but yet...I pay $1000/mo for that insurance?! Make that make sense..."

A woman is lying down, phone held above her, looking into the lens with the flat expression of somebody who has run out of energy for the topic. Across the frame sits a screenshot styled like a viral post, and the numbers in it are the hook: $6,000, then $1,000 a month, then the shrug of "make that make sense." She does not read the overlay aloud. Her own line arrives underneath it: "This is why you can't simply just say oh I have insurance I'm covered."

The mechanic here is a borrowed-outrage overlay. It is not a Comment Response in the `hooks.md` sense, because the screenshot is not a comment on the brand's own ad, so I am labelling it in my own words: **screenshot-outrage overlay**. It works on the information gap and on plain arithmetic. The two numbers do not reconcile, and the brain stays to resolve them. Processing fluency is high, the words are small and the maths is instant, while the content is genuinely surprising. That is the exact combination `hook-psychology.md` calls easy to process and hard to predict.

The original "MOMS39 - 2 - V2", launched **2026-03-14**, has spent **$27,775.42** lifetime for **1,213 leads at a $22.90 CPL**, on a **40.62% hook rate** and a **7.74% hold rate**. The copy running in this window, "MOMS39 - 2 - V2 - Copy", spent **$1,438.61** for **75 leads at a $19.18 CPL** on a **37.06% hook rate** and a 5.01% hold, with a strong **2.45% CTR**. *Verified*. This is the account's best balance of stop and cost, and its CPL sits in the brand's "good" band.

Awareness stage: **Problem Aware**, and arguably teaching toward Unaware, since a share of viewers genuinely have not done this piece of arithmetic on their own plan.

### 4. "I wish this was a joke... (health insurance)"

A woman in warm domestic light looks at the camera and exhales, the sigh of somebody about to retell an annoying phone call. She says the line flat: "I wish this was a joke." Then the video does something none of the others do. It becomes a two-hander skit. She plays herself and an agent, and the agent's answers get worse and worse: "the silver package will be $600 a month with a $7,000 deductible", then "it means you'd pay $14,000 this year before we cover anything", then a gold package at "$1,800 a month" that turns out to be "$25,000 out of pocket before we cover anything." She keeps asking the question the viewer is asking. "Is this a joke?" "This is insane. What is the point of insurance?"

Usage count: **16 distinct video ads** lifetime, carrying **$19,907.65** and **713 leads at a $27.92 CPL**. *Verified*.

This one is strategically interesting for two reasons that have nothing to do with its CPL. First, it holds people. The flagship "Moms36 - 3 - A - 2 - V4c" runs a **15.17% lifetime hold rate**, and the copy in this window ran an **11.16% hold** with a **41.25% fifteen-second retention** and a **9.7 second average play time**. Those are by far the best attention numbers in the account, and the only ones near the 12% craft floor. Second, it reaches an older woman. Spend on the "I wish this was a joke" family skews **30.4% to ages 45-54** and 9.3% to 55-64, against 14.3% and 3.6% for the account overall. *Verified*. Something about the slower, drier, more explanatory build pulls a demographic the rest of the account barely touches.

Its hook rate is fine but not top, at **39.82% lifetime** and 27.06% on the window copy, and its CPL of $27.92 sits in the brand's "not good" band. So this is a hook that under-stops relative to the POV opener but massively over-holds, and it is buying a different audience while doing it.

### 5. "Approved State List ✔️" spoken over "I'm so thankful for the mom that told me about this."

A woman in a grey tank top stands in front of a plain white screen filled edge to edge with a long list of US state names, under a header reading "Approved State List ✔️". The list is the visual event. Before you process a single word she has said, your eye is already hunting the list for your own state, which is a small act of participation almost nobody scrolls past. Her voice arrives warm and immediate: "I'm so thankful for the mom that told me about this." At 0:02 she qualifies you out loud, "If you're a mom and you live in one of these states and you're tired of paying high deductibles, high premiums for health insurance for your family, listen up."

This is a Conversational hook fused to the brand's state mechanic, and it is the cheapest lead source in the set. "moms-53 3", launched **2026-08-25**, spent **$1,761.00** in the window for **110 leads at a $16.01 CPL**, on a **35.85% hook rate** with a standout **3.15% CTR** and a **$0.67 CPC**. *Verified*. That CPL is in the brand's "good" band and the CTR is the highest of any ad in the top 20.

What makes the opener work is not the sentence, it is the list. The state roll is a self-reference device wearing a visual costume. And the spoken line does something the rest of the account never does: it credits another mother rather than the brand, which is the "mom-to-mom" register the brand context names as its own moat. *Stated* by the brand.

Usage count: this hook is new, created 2026-08-25, with one copy spun up on 2026-08-31. **2 uses.** Trajectory: just launched, climbing.

### 6. "Sharing this 2026 health insurance hack!!"

A selfie-framed woman films herself, and over her shoulder sits a screenshot of an X post from somebody complaining about their deductible. She talks straight into it with no preamble: "This is why you can't simply just say 'Oh, I have insurance, I'm covered.'" By 0:05 she has named the promise, "you need health insurance that has a $0 deductible without spending thousands", and by 0:10 the state gate lands.

The word doing the heavy lifting is "hack". It is a trigger word in the curiosity family, and it frames the whole thing as insider knowledge being passed along rather than a product being sold. "Moms43 - 5 - V1", launched **2026-06-01**, spent **$6,735.96** in the window for **243 leads at a $27.72 CPL** on a **38.91% hook rate** and an **8.05% hold rate**, with a **2.28% CTR**. Read it as a working hook with a cost problem rather than a failed opener. The hook rate clears the bar comfortably and the hold is second best in the set, but its CPM is the highest of the big spenders at **$33.23** against a $23.64 account average, and that CPM is most of why its CPL drifted into the brand's "not good" band this window.

### Pattern read across the brand's openers

Three things stand out when you hold all ten against each other.

**The account has one body and many faces.** Every single ad in the top 20 carries the identical 130-word ad copy about moms being "protectors of futures", and nearly every transcript funnels into the same state gate and the same "$0 deductible" promise within about 20 seconds. The variation is entirely in the first three seconds. That is worth naming because it means this account is, structurally, a hook-testing machine already, whether or not anyone framed it that way. The brand said as much: "Right now we're mainly on iterations of our state angle... But not a ton of creative diversity." *Stated*.

**Intensity buys the stop, calm buys the lead.** This is the sharpest thing in the data. Sort the window by opener temperature and the split is clean. The calm and aspirational openers, the POV husband hook, the two state-list ads and the school-pickup ad, delivered CPLs of **$13.29, $15.14, $16.01, $19.52, $21.56 and $24.45**. The high-distress crying openers delivered **$29.85, $34.94 and $38.09**, and the family blended to **$33.23**. Meanwhile the distress ads posted the *higher* hook rates, 57.02% and 52.40% against the flagship's 45.55%. So the ads that stop the most people produce the most expensive leads. *Inferred*, and I want to be careful about how hard that lands: the crying ads sit in a paused ad set at lower spend, and delivery differences could be doing some of this. But the direction is consistent across six ads and it matches the mechanism in `hook-psychology.md` and `emotional-delivery-and-timing.md`, which both say a high-intensity negative open on a cold audience reads as an ambush, and that a threat opened without relief encodes anxiety against the brand rather than desire for it. The women watching are mid-scroll in a school pickup line, not in problem-solving mode.

**Hook rate and hold rate are pulling in opposite directions, and no ad has both.** The best stoppers hold nobody: the flagship POV ad holds 3.30%, the toddler ad 2.30%. The best holders under-stop: the skit holds 11.16% to 15.17% on a 27.06% to 39.82% hook rate. Nothing in the account clears 40% hook and 10% hold at the same time. Whether that matters is genuinely open, because the lead form does not require a long watch, and the account's cheapest leads come from a 2.39% hold ad. It is in open loops for that reason.

**Notable absences.** Against the `hooks.md` taxonomy the account never runs Authority, never runs Investment, never runs a true Comment Response using its own ad comments, never runs Comparison, never runs Give Me Time and never runs Viral Product. Authority is the loudest gap given the category is trust-sensitive and the niche organic below shows a doctor outperforming everything else in the feed by a factor of seven. There is also no promo creative at all in the set, which is not an oversight: the brand's compliance rules ban the words "cheap", "discount" and "guaranteed" outright. *Stated*. So the usual promo-hook exclusion this audit applies simply had nothing to exclude.

## Brand hooks with weak attention signal

Only one hook in the account earns a true weak-hook verdict on spend plus hook rate. The rest of what looks weak turns out to be something else, and the prompt's four-way split is doing real work here.

**True weak hook — "if you're a mom and your deductible is insane listen to this".** The original ad has had **$17,723.91** of lifetime spend, which is more than enough delivery to read, and it has held a **26.95% hook rate** across **810,763 impressions**. That is under the 30% craft floor and roughly 19 points under the flagship POV hook running in the same account to the same audience. Its two copies in this window did not fix it, landing at 29.43% and 25.16%, the lowest hook rate of any ad in the account spending over $200. Its closest control is the "My deductible is $6000" screenshot ad, which opens on the same pain with the same kind of overlay and pulls **40.62%**. Same problem, same audience, same body, 14 points of hook rate apart. *Verified*. The diagnosis is job one: the frame is a tight crop of a sad face with a text block over it, and there is no motion, no anomaly and no list to scan, so nothing wins the involuntary glance before the words have to. Verdict: **true weak hook**, not fatigue, since it opened weak in April and has never been strong.

**Not fatigue, despite the volume — the POV husband hook.** This is the one I expected to find worn out. It has been used **20 times** across five months and is the most repeated line in the account, which is exactly the profile where juice gets squeezed dry. It has not happened. Lifetime hook rate is **45.76%** and the window hook rate is **45.55%**, essentially flat, and the newest copies from 2026-08-14 and 2026-08-27 came in at 40.15%, 42.41%, 43.41%, 43.08% and 32.19%. So there is mild softening in the copies but no decay in the parent. Verdict: **not fatigued yet on hook rate**. Recorded so next month's audit has a baseline to compare against, since this is the number that will show fatigue first.

**Working hooks with a downstream failure, routed out of this section.** Three ads posted alarming CPLs that have nothing to do with their openers, and lumping them in with weak hooks would have been wrong. "Moms Nahuel WV#1 - V9 - Copy" in the CBO OTP campaign shows a **$273.15 CPL**, but it recorded **690 link clicks and only 63 landing page views**. "moms-63 3e - Copy" shows **$78.54** on 304 link clicks and **28 landing page views**. "moms-63 2b - Copy" shows **$96.42** on 287 link clicks and **23 landing page views**. "moms-53 3 - Copy" recorded **511 link clicks, 9 landing page views and zero leads**. Every one of those four points at `https://go.healthformoms.co/save/`. The versions of the same creative pointing at `https://www.healthformoms.co/save/` behave normally, and one of them, "moms-63 2b", is the cheapest lead source in the whole account at a **$13.29 CPL**. *Verified*. Same hook, same video file, two destinations, wildly different outcomes. That is a landing page or tracking problem, not a hook problem, and it goes to open loops.

**Inconclusive.** The two highest hook rates in the account, 64.15% and 52.71%, sit on **$138.74** and **$47.38** of spend. Too thin to call in either direction. Named here so they are not mistaken for proven winners.

## Competitor hooks

**No competitor hooks were read this cycle.** This is a blank, not an omission.

Parker's external ad library holds zero tracked brands for Health For Moms. I checked it directly rather than assuming: `search_competitor_facebook_ads` with mode `brands` returned `totalCount: 0` on 2026-09-03. *Verified*. The competitor branch was deferred at the team's request during the Phase 0 build, and adding rivals inside the Parker app backfills this section without any other work being redone.

The gap is worth sizing honestly, because the brand context also records that the team **did not name any competitors** when asked. *Stated*. So there is no locked competitor set to hold constant across cycles yet, which is the one thing this audit's rotation rule assumes exists. The brand context does describe the competitive field in tiers, naming employer-provided insurance as the real default rival and generic insurance lead-generation and comparison platforms as the direct tier, but describing a field is not the same as reading a library, and no first frame from any of those brands was inspected. ### Pattern read across niche organic

**Authority is the biggest single gap between this feed and the brand's account.** The top organic video in the category by a wide margin is a doctor doing investigative work on camera, and the brand has never run an Authority hook. It sits on the brand's own testing wish list under "Authority" and remains untested. *Stated*.

**Organic in this category opens quiet and specific, not loud and sad.** Not one of these nine opens on crying. The strongest ones open on a calm voice saying something structurally shocking, a doctor placing a call, a mother stating flatly that her family of four has no insurance, a hand panning down a bill. The emotional intensity lives in the *fact*, not in the delivery. That is the same Zone 1 and Zone 3 pattern the brand's own paid numbers point at, arriving from a completely independent source.

**The pregnancy and new-baby window is where this category's organic attention concentrates.** Seven of the nine are anchored to pregnancy, delivery costs or a newborn. The brand's paid creative touches this only glancingly, in the single "newborn son's hospital stay" ad, which is also one of its most expensive. The organic feed suggests the trigger moment matters more than the crisis.

**The documentary artefact is a recurring visual hook.** The itemised bill, the state list, the price written on screen as it is spoken, the screenshot. Across both the organic feed and the brand's own best paid work, the openers that perform put a *document* in frame rather than a face alone. The brand's own state list ad is the in-house version of this and it produced a $16.01 CPL.

## Open loops

**1. Why does the account's cheapest attention produce its most expensive leads?**
The crying-mom openers stop more people than anything else in the account, at 57.02% and 52.40% hook rates, and they produce leads at $29.85 and $38.09 against a $22.10 account average, while the calm POV and state-list openers stop fewer people and deliver leads at $13.29 to $16.01. Pull: **surprise**. It fired because the ranking flipped when I sorted by hook rate versus by CPL, and the ad with the best stop rate in the whole account sits in the brand's worst CPL tier. *Question:* What is happening between the stop and the form fill that makes a high-distress opener cost so much more per lead? This matters because the account is currently building more of the distress lane, six ads and climbing, and if intensity is actively working against lead cost then that production is buying the wrong thing. Territory: **Messaging**.

**2. What are the openers that hold people actually doing that the openers that stop people are not?**
No ad in the account clears both a 40% hook rate and a 10% hold rate. The skit holds 11.16% to 15.17% but stops at 27.06%. The flagship POV stops 45.55% and holds 3.30%. Pull: **tension**. Two ads in the same account, same body copy, same audience, and they are good at opposite halves of the job. *Question:* What is it about the skit's opening that keeps people watching nine seconds longer than anything else here? Answering it would tell the team whether the two jobs can be combined in one opener or whether they are genuinely separate creative bets. Territory: **Messaging**.

**3. Does hold rate matter at all for this brand's lead form?**
The account's cheapest lead comes from an ad with a 2.39% hold rate and a 3.38 second average play time, and its best-holding ad sits in the worst CPL band. The craft standard says a hold under 12% is a problem. Pull: **tension**. The brand's own numbers and the general method disagree, and only one of them can be right for this account. *Question:* How much of the lead volume here comes from people who watched past three seconds? This changes what every future script optimises for, because if the answer is "almost none" then the body of the ad is far less important than the industry default assumes. Territory: **Product**.

**4. Who is the older woman the skit is reaching, and is she a persona nobody has named?**
The "I wish this was a joke" family puts **30.4% of its spend on women aged 45-54** and 9.3% on 55-64, against 14.3% and 3.6% for the account overall. Nothing else in the account does this. Pull: **gap**. There is a demographic showing up in the delivery data that no creative in the account is deliberately speaking to. *Question:* Who is the 45 to 55 year old woman that this slower, drier format is pulling in, and what is she actually looking for? The brand already said it wants to test "All different ages of moms with ages of kids", and this is delivery data pointing at a specific age band before anyone targeted it. Territory: **Personas**.

**5. What would an Authority opener do in this account?**
The single best-performing video in the entire niche library is a doctor in a lab coat cold-calling hospitals, at 6.2 million views, roughly seven times the next video. The brand has never run an Authority hook and lists it as untested. Pull: **gap**. There is a proven format sitting in the brand's own category, in its own mining library, that the account has never once tried. *Question:* What happens to hook rate and CPL when a credentialed voice opens the ad instead of a peer? The category is trust-sensitive and the brand's whole model routes to licensed advisors, so credibility is an asset it already owns and never shows. Territory: **Creators and talent**.

**6. Why does the same creative perform so differently across two landing destinations?**
Four ads pointing at `go.healthformoms.co` recorded 690, 511, 304 and 287 link clicks against 63, 9, 28 and 23 landing page views, with CPLs of $273.15, no leads at all, $78.54 and $96.42. The identical video files pointing at `www.healthformoms.co` produced the account's cheapest leads. Pull: **surprise**. Same hook, same file, same audience, and one destination behaves as though nearly every click evaporates. *Question:* Where are the clicks going on the `go.` destination? Until this is understood, every hook tested in the CBO OTP campaign is being graded on numbers that do not reflect the hook. Territory: **Product**.

**7. Why does organic in this category never open on crying, when the brand's paid does it constantly?**
Not one of the nine niche organic videos opens on visible distress, and the strongest ones open calm and let the fact carry the shock. The brand's newest paid lane does the opposite. Pull: **pattern**. The same signal turned up independently in the brand's own CPL data and in an unrelated organic feed. *Question:* What does a calm delivery of a shocking fact do to a viewer that a distressed delivery of the same fact does not? Territory: **Messaging**.

**8. Is the brand's most-used hook actually near its ceiling, or has nobody found the ceiling yet?**
The POV husband line has run 20 times across five months and $87,991.09, and its hook rate has not moved, sitting at 45.76% lifetime against 45.55% this window, though the newest copies drift down toward 32% to 43%. Pull: **curiosity**. Twenty uses of one line with no measurable decay is not what the fatigue model predicts. *Question:* How much further can one hook line be repeated in this account before attention falls off? The answer decides whether the team keeps milking a known winner or starts spending its limited production capacity on net-new openers. Territory: **Messaging**.
